  \begin{abstract}
  \noindent 
  Lattice gauge theory configuration generation via Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) suffers from critical slowing down, particularly in the topological charge mode, which can freeze entirely at small lattice spacing. Hot or cold random seeds do not spread topological charge across its full range, and the cost of tunneling between sectors leaves them unexplored. A correctly distributed starting configuration removes this limitation. This paper describes a diffusion model which generates HMC starting seeds with the correct topological distribution in 2D U(1) and U(2), performing an inverse renormalization group step that lifts a coarse configuration to a fine seed. The model directly transports the topological charge from the coarse configuration, and then supplies plausible UV structure, the fast modes HMC is able to correct. The method reaches $\beta \approx 1660$ at $128\times128$ from a base of $\beta \approx 3.5$ at $8\times8$ with topology transport exact throughout --- four times the largest volume in the training set, where the seed sits within one part in $10^{6}$ of the exact plaquette before a single trajectory, against one part in $10^{3}$ for a cold start. Seed quality away from trained couplings degrades with coupling distance, and we show that what has to be covered by training is the coupling rather than the volume: checkpoints differing only in coupling range deliver seeds 4--10$\times$ closer to exact, in the same order at both $32\times32$ and $64\times64$, even though none of them is trained above $32\times32$ and the wider ones' extra coverage was added entirely at $8\times8$. These seeded chains are thermalized ($\langle Q^2\rangle$ within $1\sigma$, all tested Wilson loops within $2.5\sigma$) within 400 trajectories, compared to 6-600$\sigma$ failures for cold/hot starts over the same budget. Within 10\% of a trained coupling the seed thermalizes in as few as 3-10 trajectories where classical starts never do; the advantage shrinks, but does not vanish, as the coupling moves further from training coverage.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Lattice Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) is a non-perturbative discretization of the strong force in Quantum Field Theory (QFT), used to investigate strong interactions and hadron structure.
These investigations are done by generating configurations, which are 4D grids with an SU(3) matrix on every edge of the lattice and a dynamical fermion field. Each configuration has a corresponding action, and configurations must be sampled from the Boltzmann distribution in that action,
\begin{equation}
    p(\phi) \propto e^{-S[\phi]},
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the configuration and the missing normalization is the partition function $Z$. Once a batch of configurations is generated, Monte Carlo methods are used to compute expected values of observables without evaluating the infinite-dimensional path integrals that QFT formally requires.

The central claim of this paper is that a diffusion model can generate better seed configurations for Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) than the conventional cold or hot starts, specifically at couplings where HMC's own topological-charge sampling is expensive or frozen. A seed configuration is the initial state $\phi_0$ from which HMC runs. The chain is then evolved (\S\ref{sec:setup}) until it has thermalized, meaning its measured observables agree with the theory's Boltzmann-distributed expectation values within statistical error. A cold start sets every link to the identity; a hot start draws every link independently and uniformly from the gauge group. Both are cheap to construct, but each carries no information about the target distribution, so an HMC chain begun from either one must be run for a length of time ($t_{therm}$) before its samples are usable. This paper shows that this thermalization time can be reduced from hundreds of trajectories, or from never in a fixed budget, to a small number (or zero), by instead starting the chain from a configuration produced by a diffusion model.

Configurations are generated by Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC, \S\ref{sec:setup}),
whose cost grows close to exponentially as the lattice spacing shrinks toward
the continuum limit --- ``critical slowing down'' \cite{schaefer2011} --- the
regime most useful for physical predictions. Generation separately suffers
from topological freezing, where large action barriers leave much of the
topological-charge sector unexplored \cite{luscher2011}, so generated batches
no longer follow the expected distribution of topological charge
(\S\ref{sec:freezing}). Prior mitigations such as mass preconditioning for
dynamical fermions \cite{hasenbusch2001,hasenbuschjansen2003} address the
fermionic sector's cost rather than topological freezing, and do not apply to
the pure-gauge theories studied here. This paper instead builds a diffusion
model performing an inverse renormalization-group (RG) transform, lifting a
configuration at a large, cheap lattice spacing (where HMC is not frozen) to
one at a small spacing (where it is), and uses the result as an HMC seed
rather than as a final product.

Because full QCD (4D SU(3) with dynamical fermions) is prohibitively
expensive, we use simpler theories that retain the relevant characteristics:
2D U(1) and U(2). U(1) is abelian and computationally simple, one scalar per
link ($\theta$ for $e^{i\theta}$), the natural 2D analogue of
electrodynamics; the characteristic it shares with full QCD and that this
study exploits is a non-trivial topological charge. U(2) is a non-abelian step
beyond it that still carries one, via
$$U(2) = \frac{U(1) \times SU(2)}{Z_2}.$$
Since SU(2) is topologically trivial in 2D (only $Q=0$; \S\ref{sec:setup}),
the topological machinery built for U(1) extends directly. Both theories are
exactly solvable, so output can be validated against a closed form rather than
against another uncertain simulation.

Machine learning has been applied to this problem with increasing effort,
largely through normalizing flows \cite{albergo2019} and diffusion models.
Wang, Aarts and Zhou first cast the reverse diffusion process as a
stochastic-quantization scheme for lattice field theory \cite{wang2024}; Zhu
et al.\ applied a diffusion model directly to 2D U(1) configurations
\cite{zhu2024} and extended it to physics-conditioned sampling across
couplings \cite{zhu2025}; Aarts et al.\ generalize to non-abelian 2D
U(2)/SU(2) with an equivariant architecture \cite{aarts2026}, the setting of
this paper's U(2) extension. Those works generate configurations directly and
are evaluated on how well the raw ensemble matches the target. We instead hand
the model's output to HMC (with a topological winding update) as a seed and
thermalize further, so the comparison is the trajectory cost of the
\emph{delivered} ensemble against classical starts under the same sampler.
This matters because a general limitation of generative models applied to
gauge fields is that the raw ensemble tends to place too much mass on sectors
of unphysically high $|Q|$, and a headline $\langle Q^2\rangle$ close to exact
can hide sector-by-sector disagreement. That is the motivation for the central
design choice: rather than asking the model to reproduce $P(Q)$, the
topological charge is transported directly from the coarse configuration
(\S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}), making the delivered $Q$ exact by construction
and removing the failure mode regardless of what the model has learned about
topology. \S\ref{sec:conclusions} returns to this.

The paper is organized as follows. \S\ref{sec:setup} introduces 2D U(1) and U(2), how observables are defined, the standard lattice configuration generation tool HMC, and the limitations of topological freezing and critical slowing down, together with the classical winding updates used as this paper's baseline. \S\ref{sec:method} walks through the diffusion model's methodology: topological charge transport, the scoring network, and the reverse diffusion process itself. \S\ref{sec:seedquality} compares the diffusion seed against cold- and hot-started HMC with the winding update, in both observable distributions and trajectory cost. \S\ref{sec:conclusions} summarizes the result and discusses what does, and does not, carry over to full QCD.

\section{2D U(1) and U(2) Gauge Theories, and Their Simulation}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Gauge Links}
The basis of these lattice gauge theories are gauge links ($U_{x,\mu}$) between neighboring lattice sites. In 2D U(1), each gauge link is an element of U(1), i.e.\ a complex phase, $U_{x,\mu} = e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}$. In 2D U(2), each gauge link is an element of U(2) and can be represented by a unitary $2\times2$ complex matrix. For computational convenience we instead use the decomposition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:u2-link} below, writing any U(2) element as the product of an SU(2) matrix and a complex phase; since SU(2) is isomorphic to the group of unit quaternions, five real scalars describe a U(2) gauge link.
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,\mu} = e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}
q_1 + i q_4 & q_3 + i q_2 \\
-q_3 + i q_2 & q_1 - i q_4
\end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:u2-link}
\end{equation}
subject to the unit-quaternion constraint $q_1^2 + q_2^2 + q_3^2 + q_4^2 = 1$.

These gauge links are directional: $U_{x,\mu}$ represents a horizontal link running left to right, and $U_{x,\nu}$ a vertical link running bottom to top. Conjugation reverses the direction, so $U_{x,\mu}^{\dagger}$ is the same link traversed right to left \cite{gattringerlang2010}.
\subsection{Plaquettes/Wilson Loops}
The physical observables of these gauge theories are all written in terms of link variables. The building block of every observable is the plaquette,
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,p} \equiv U_{x,\mu} U_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} U_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu}^\dagger U_{x,\nu}^\dagger,
    \label{eq:plaquette}
\end{equation}
the smallest closed loop on the lattice, and, like any closed loop, a gauge-invariant quantity. Products of adjacent plaquettes form larger Wilson loops, which are also gauge invariant and serve as observables.
In U(1), the plaquette can be written directly in terms of the link angles $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{x,p} \equiv \theta_{x,\mu} + \theta_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} - \theta_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu} - \theta_{x,\nu}.
    \label{eq:theta-plaquette}
\end{equation}
Because U(1) is abelian, this is simply a sum, and the order of summation is irrelevant. U(2) is non-abelian, so in Eq.~\eqref{eq:plaquette} the order of matrix multiplication is significant.

\subsection{Topological Charge}
In 2D U(1), the topological charge $Q$ is defined as the wrapped sum of all plaquette angles:
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{x} \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\sin(\theta_{x,p})}{\cos(\theta_{x,p})}\right).
    \label{eq:q-u1}
\end{equation}
The inverse tangent wraps each plaquette angle back to $(-\pi,\pi]$. Without this wrapping, the sum over plaquettes would always vanish identically, since every link variable is a summand once in its original direction and once with the opposite sign, so all terms would cancel. The wrapping instead adds a compensating factor of $2\pi n$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, to any plaquette angle that would otherwise fall outside the principal branch, so dividing the sum by $2\pi$ leaves $Q$ an exact integer (up to floating-point round-off accumulated across the lattice).
In 2D U(2), $Q$ is defined analogously,
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sum_{x} \alpha_x,
    \label{eq:q-u2}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_x$ the wrapped determinant-plaquette phase,
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_x=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\sin(\arg\det U_{x,p})}{\cos(\arg\det U_{x,p})}\right).
    \label{eq:alpha-x}
\end{equation}
As before, the inverse tangent constrains the angle to $(-\pi,\pi]$. Recall the link definition of Eq.~\eqref{eq:u2-link}; since SU(2) matrices have unit determinant,
\begin{equation}
    \det(U_{x,p})=e^{2i\theta_{x,p}},
    \label{eq:det-plaquette}
\end{equation}
because when the plaquette is assembled from Eq.~\eqref{eq:plaquette}, the U(1) phase factors on each link commute out of the product and combine into exactly the U(1) plaquette sum of Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-plaquette} -- here $\theta_{x,\mu}$ denoting the U(1) component of the U(2) link variables. So the SU(2) component of a U(2) link contributes nothing at all to the topological charge, which is why SU(2) is considered topologically trivial.
\subsection{Definition of Action in 2D U(1) and U(2)}
In 2D U(1), the Wilson action is
\begin{equation}
    S = -\beta \sum_{x}\cos(\theta_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:action-u1}
\end{equation}
gauge invariant since it depends only on plaquette angles. Physically, the plaquette measures the local field strength (flux through the elementary cell), and the Boltzmann weight $e^{-S}$ favors configurations of low field strength.
In 2D U(2), the action is
\begin{equation}
    S = -\beta \sum_{x} \frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}(U_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:action-u2}
\end{equation}
again gauge invariant because it depends only on the gauge-invariant plaquette, not on individual links.

\subsection{Topological Charge Freezing}
\label{sec:freezing}
Topological freezing is an obstruction for HMC that worsens toward the continuum limit: as the lattice spacing shrinks, $\beta$ grows, and the action becomes more sensitive to changes in the plaquette angles. Because the topological charge is a global property of the whole lattice, it is exponentially unlikely for the local changes an HMC trajectory makes to change it. Since the 2D U(2) topological charge (Eq.~\eqref{eq:q-u2}) depends only on the U(1) (determinant) component of the U(2) gauge links, the mechanism below is derived for 2D U(1) and carries over to U(2) unchanged.
Because $Q$ is defined through wrapping, it can only change if some $\theta_{x,p}$ is pushed past $(-\pi,\pi]$, adding one unit of topological charge. At large $\beta$ every plaquette angle is clustered near $\theta_{x,p}\simeq0$, so changing $Q$ requires an intermediate configuration with some $\theta_{x,p}$ near $\pm\pi$, where the wrap contributes an extra $2\pi$. Reaching that intermediate state through a purely local update costs $\Delta S = 2\beta$, so the corresponding HMC step is accepted with probability $\sim e^{-2\beta}$, exponentially suppressed in $\beta$, which is the root cause of topological freezing in HMC.

\subsection{Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC)}
HMC proposes new configurations $\phi$ by drawing an auxiliary momentum field $\pi$ (one component per link) from a Gaussian distribution and evolving the pair under Hamiltonian dynamics,
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,\mu} \pi_{\mu}^2(x) + S[\phi], \qquad \mu\in\{1,2\},
\end{equation}
discretized by the Omelyan integrator for U(1) and by an exponential-map leapfrog integrator for the group-valued U(2)/SU(2) fields \cite{omelyan2003}. The entire resulting trajectory, not a single local change, is proposed as one Metropolis step and accepted with probability $P_{\mathrm{acc}} = \min(1, e^{-\Delta H})$, $\Delta H = H'-H$ between the evolved and initial Hamiltonians; this Metropolis--Hastings acceptance is what guarantees sampling from the Boltzmann distribution despite the integrator's discretization error. After a fixed number of burn-in trajectories, configurations are kept every $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$ trajectories -- too small a $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$ leaves kept configurations correlated -- and observables are Monte Carlo estimated over these decorrelated configurations \cite{duane1987,neal2011}.

\subsection{Instanton Updates}
\label{sec:instanton_updates_classical}
In 2D U(1), a method called winding Hybrid Monte Carlo (wHMC) \cite{albandea2021} has seen recent success in delaying the topological-charge freezing barrier to much higher $\beta$ than standard HMC. It works by proposing an instanton field, a field carrying precisely one unit of topological charge, as a global update, and accepting or rejecting it with the same Metropolis criterion as standard HMC. This method is used throughout this paper as the classical baseline, since it is a stronger comparison than topologically frozen standard HMC. It does not eliminate topological freezing, only delays it (Table~\ref{tab:u1_frozen}, \S\ref{sec:cost}).

In 2D U(2), an analogous instanton construction is available because $Q$ depends only on the abelian determinant sector $\psi \equiv \arg\det U = \mathrm{wrap}(2\phi)$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:q-u2}), which the SU(2) sector integrates out of the action exactly, plaquette by plaquette against Haar measure -- a genuine 2D solvability statement with no analogue in $D>2$ or with fermions -- giving the closed-form marginal weight $w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta)=2I_1(z)/z$, $z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2)$ (derived in Appendix~\ref{app:su2-marginal}; tabulated in Table~\ref{tab:u2-exact-observables}).
\label{sec:instanton}%
This decomposition supports two distinct global moves, and only one of them can reach every sector. In the split representation $U=e^{i\phi}q$, a purely central phase shift $\phi\to\phi+\lambda$ commutes with the SU(2) sector entirely and is therefore free to apply; it moves $\psi=2\phi$ by $2\lambda$ and $Q$ by exactly two, at the same cost $\Delta S = 2\pi^2\beta/V$ as the U(1) instanton itself. This will be referred to as the \emph{even-only} move: cheap and unconditional, but structurally incapable of changing the sector's parity, so it can never reach an odd $Q$ relative to the starting configuration. Reaching an odd sector instead requires (i) proposing $\psi'=\psi\pm\lambda$, changing $Q$ by exactly $\pm1$; (ii) accepting with the Metropolis ratio of the \emph{marginal} action alone, $\min(1,e^{-\Delta S_{\det}})$, discarding the SU(2) sector from the acceptance decision since it is about to be resampled; and (iii) resampling $q\sim p(q\mid\psi')$ exactly by Creutz--Kennedy--Pendleton heatbath (Appendix~\ref{app:su2-marginal}) \cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985}. This will be referred to as the \emph{marginal} move: the only one of the two that is odd-capable, at a real per-trajectory cost. Both preserve the Boltzmann distribution exactly, and both are used as classical baselines throughout this paper (\S\ref{sec:cost}).

The gap between them is not academic. At $L=64$, $\beta=416.524$ -- the ladder's top rung and the hardest coupling in this study -- 400 cold-started trajectories give \textbf{zero} accepted parity flips under the even-only move ($P(\mathrm{odd})=0$ against exact $0.4929$, $z=-78.9$), against $7661$ flips under the marginal move ($P(\mathrm{odd})=0.4939$, $z=+0.17$; further couplings in Table~\ref{tab:u2-joint-marginal}). \S\ref{sec:instanton_apply} describes how the reverse diffusion process instead applies the same instanton fields deterministically, without a Metropolis step, to enforce exact topology transport regardless of this mobility gap.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:method-overview}

The method is organized around one inverse renormalization-group step: a coarse configuration at size $L_c\times L_c$ and coupling $\beta_c$ is lifted to a fine configuration at $L_f=2L_c$, $\beta_f\approx4\beta_c$ (the exact match is derived in \S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}), with the fine configuration's topological charge transported \emph{exactly} from the coarse one and only the remaining ultraviolet structure supplied by a learned model and a short classical tail (Fig.~\ref{fig:method_schematic}). There are two phases, sharing the same coarse-to-fine relationship in two different roles.

\textbf{Training} builds one dataset per coupling covered and is done once, offline. A reference fine ensemble is generated by HMC (\S\ref{sec:setup}) at each training coupling; every fine configuration is naively blocked down to its coarse partner by the deterministic map of \S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}, which pairs the two exactly ($Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}=Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ by construction) and gives every training example a definite conditioning target. At couplings where honest HMC sampling of the fine ensemble is itself frozen in $Q$ (\S\ref{sec:freezing}), a fixed fraction of the fine ensemble is additionally augmented with instanton-shifted copies at other topological charges (end of \S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}), so training pairs are not confined to the trivial sector the frozen HMC ensemble alone would supply. The score network (\S\ref{sec:scoring-network}) is then trained by denoising score matching against noised versions of these fine configurations, conditioned throughout on their coarse partner.

\textbf{Generation} is what is actually timed and compared against classical starts in \S\ref{sec:seedquality}: given a \emph{new} coarse configuration -- one the target study needs a fine seed for, not one seen in training -- the reverse diffusion process of \S\ref{sec:reverse_diffusion} runs from pure noise on the fine lattice, conditioned on the given coarse configuration throughout, with the coarse topological charge enforced exactly by a deterministic projection applied during and after sampling. For U(2), the naive inverse-blocked SU(2) sector is then replaced by an exact conditional heatbath sweep at the now-fixed determinant sector (\S\ref{sec:instanton_apply}), since that sector requires no learned model at all. The raw output of this process is not treated as a final product but handed to HMC as a starting configuration: a short local rethermalization tail (topology-preserving heatbath/overrelaxation sweeps) relaxes any remaining ultraviolet mismatch before the resulting chain enters the comparison against cold- and hot-started HMC in \S\ref{sec:seedquality}.

\subsection{Diffusion Models}
\label{sec:diffusion-models}
Diffusion models generate samples by learning to reverse a noising process. Formally, the forward process is an It\^o stochastic differential equation (SDE), $dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW$, that transforms the data distribution $p_0$ into a tractable reference distribution $p_T$ over a fixed time horizon $t\in[0,T]$. The key theoretical fact making generation possible is due to Anderson \cite{anderson1982}: this forward SDE has a reverse-time companion process,
$$dx = \big[f(x,t) - g(t)^2\nabla_x \log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar W,$$
which reproduces the same marginal distributions $p_t$ at every $t$ when run backward from $T$ to $0$. Sampling from $p_0$ therefore reduces to simulating this reverse process, and the only unknown quantity it requires is the score, $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ --- the gradient of the log-density of the noised distribution at each intermediate time $t$, not of the clean data distribution alone.

The score cannot be computed directly, since $p_t$ is a marginal over the entire (unknown) data distribution. Denoising score matching \cite{vincent2011} sidesteps this: because the conditional transition kernel $p(x_t\mid x_0)$ induced by the forward SDE is known in closed form, a network $s_\theta(x_t,t)$ can instead be trained to match the conditional score, $\mathbb E|s_\theta(x_t,t)-\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid x_0)|^2$, which is provably equivalent to matching the true marginal score up to a constant independent of $\theta$. Trained this way, $s_\theta$ acts as the drift correction in the reverse-time SDE above, steering a sample from pure noise back toward the data distribution. The same score function also parameterizes Langevin dynamics targeting $p_t$, which is what unifies score-based models, denoising diffusion, and annealed Langevin sampling into a single SDE framework \cite{song2021}; in practice the reverse SDE is simulated by discretizing $[0,T]$ into a finite noise schedule and applying a numerical integrator, often combining an SDE step with an auxiliary Langevin correction step at each noise level.

This standard construction assumes $x\in\mathbb R^n$ and a Gaussian transition kernel. Neither holds for our data: lattice link angles are valued on a compact, periodic domain rather than $\mathbb R^n$, so both the forward process and the score it induces must instead be built from the domain's own transition kernel.

\subsection{Naive Blocking}
\label{sec:naive-blocking}
To start the noising process and train the score network, a fine configuration is generated at a small $\beta$ where HMC is not completely frozen and has a finite interval time to produce independent configurations, even for the slow modes. A coarse configuration is then determined completely by the relation
$$\theta_x'(X,Y) = \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_x(2X,2Y) + \theta_x(2X+1,2Y)\big)$$
$$\theta_y'(X,Y) = \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_y(2X,2Y) + \theta_y(2X,2Y+1)\big)$$

where $\theta'$ is the coarse-lattice link angle ($e^{i\theta'}$ the coarse link variable) and $\theta$ the corresponding fine-lattice link angle.
This blocking ensures $Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} = Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$, and that the coarse plaquette angle equals the wrapped sum of its four constituent fine sub-plaquette angles.

Each coarse coupling is fixed by the exact matching condition
$$\frac{I_1(\beta_c)}{I_0(\beta_c)} = \left[\frac{I_1(\beta_f)}{I_0(\beta_f)}\right]^{4},$$
derived in this work from the exact solvability of 2D Wilson theory
\cite{migdal1975,drouffezuber1983}. Because 2D pure gauge theory is exactly solvable,
plaquette angles in the fine Wilson theory are i.i.d., so the coarse cell's
density is the four-fold wrapped convolution of the fine single-plaquette
density -- exact in character space, $r_q \to r_q^4$ for every $q$, but not
reproducible by any single Wilson coupling for all $q$ simultaneously.
$\beta_c$ is therefore solved by matching only the
first character coefficient -- the boxed equation above. Its large-$\beta$
expansion, $I_1/I_0\approx 1-1/(2\beta)$, recovers the familiar tree-level
$\beta_c \approx \beta_f/4$.

At high $\beta$ the fine ensemble used to build training pairs is itself
frozen in $Q$ (\S\ref{sec:freezing}): once $\langle Q^2\rangle\ll1$, honest
HMC sampling returns configurations overwhelmingly at $Q=0$, so a training
set built purely from HMC output at a fixed high coupling would never show
the network what a nonzero-$Q$ configuration's local structure looks like at
that coupling -- exactly the coverage the network needs, since at deployment
a coarse input can carry any $Q$ and the transport step of
\S\ref{sec:reverse_diffusion} will impose it regardless of what the network
has seen in training. This coverage is manufactured directly rather than
sampled: for a fixed fraction of the fine ensemble at each high-$\beta$
training rung, a topological charge shift is drawn \emph{uniformly} at
random from a small integer range, independent of $\beta$ and of the fine
ensemble's own (collapsing) $P(Q)$, and the corresponding number of copies of
the smooth unit-winding instanton field (\S\ref{sec:instanton}) is added
directly to the plaquette angles; the shifted configuration is then relaxed
locally at fixed topology (an exact conditional sweep, \S\ref{sec:instanton_apply}, for the U(2) case) to remove the strain the shift itself introduces, without changing $Q$. This trades the correct sector \emph{frequencies} of the closed-form $P(Q)$ for
uniform sector \emph{coverage}; \S\ref{sec:conclusions} discusses what that
choice does and does not establish, and how it might generalize to theories
where $P(Q)$ is not solvable.

\subsection{Scoring Network}
\label{sec:scoring-network}
Each configuration is treated as a vector $\vec{\theta}$ with $2L^2$ components, one $x$- and one $y$-link angle per lattice site. Training proceeds by denoising score matching on the fine field only, using a wrapped Gaussian transition kernel:
$$K(\theta;\hat\theta,\sigma_t)=\frac{1}{\sigma_t\sqrt{2\pi}} \sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\theta-\hat{\theta} + 2\pi r)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right)$$
where $\hat\theta$ is the clean (unnoised) link angle and $\theta$ the noised one; as $\sigma_t$ grows this approaches the uniform distribution on the circle. The noising process treats each link as an independent variable, so the joint transition kernel over the whole configuration is the product of this single-link kernel over all $2L^2$ links.

The architecture is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}. The network's inputs are only gauge-invariant
channels of the noisy fine field, along with gauge-invariant
channels of the coarse field upsampled onto the fine grid. Upsampling here
is nearest-neighbor replication, not a learned deconvolution: the coarse
lattice has half the linear size of the fine one, so each coarse site
corresponds to one $2\times2$ block of fine sites under the blocking map of
\S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}, and every gauge-invariant coarse-field channel
(e.g.\ the coarse plaquette angle) is simply copied onto all four fine sites
of its block. This gives the coarse conditioning field the same spatial
resolution as the noisy fine field being denoised, so the two can be
concatenated channel-wise and fed through the same convolutional stack
without any further interpolation.
The noise
level $\sigma_t$ and coupling $\beta$ enter through a sinusoidal embedding
feeding per-block Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018},
which rescales and shifts each convolutional block's activations by a
learned function of that embedding rather than by concatenating it as an
extra spatial channel. Additionally, the
spatial mean of the coarse conditioning channels is additionally
projected into this same embedding, so every block has direct access to a
genuinely global quantity: the mean of the raw (wrapped) coarse plaquette
angle equals $2\pi Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}/V$ exactly, by definition of $Q$.
A convolutional network with a bounded kernel size cannot reconstruct a lattice-wide sum such as $Q$, so FiLM supplies it directly rather than requiring the network to approximate it through depth.

The Wilson action also has $90^\circ$-rotation, reflection, and charge-conjugation ($Q\to-Q$) symmetries that are not structurally built in. Instead, training draws a uniformly random element of this 16-element symmetry group and applies it to the fine configuration before blocking, noising, and training.

The network does not output a link-valued score directly: it predicts one scalar $h_p(x,y)$ per plaquette, and the link-valued score is assembled by the lattice curl,
\begin{align}
  s_x(x,y) &= h_p(x,y) - h_p(x,y-1), \label{eq:curl-x}\\
  s_y(x,y) &= h_p(x-1,y) - h_p(x,y). \label{eq:curl-y}
\end{align}
This is the general form of the gradient of any function defined as a sum of functions of plaquettes: each link belongs to exactly two plaquettes, with opposite orientation, so $\partial\theta_p/\partial\theta_\ell\in\{+1,-1,0\}$ and only two terms in the sum survive. Because $h_p$ is itself a function of gauge-invariant inputs only, no choice of network weights can produce a non-gauge-covariant, plaquette-local score.

The regression target of the score network is $\nabla_\theta \log K(\theta;\hat\theta,\sigma_t)$, the score of the wrapped Gaussian transition kernel above, evaluated at the given noise level $\sigma_t$.

\subsection{Reverse Diffusion Model}
\label{sec:reverse_diffusion}

Generation runs the reverse-time SDE directly on the U(1) link field $\theta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:charge_transport} summarizes how topology is held fixed throughout it), and the noising process of \S\ref{sec:scoring-network} fixes the general forward/reverse pair of \S\ref{sec:diffusion-models} to one specific instance: since the wrapped-Gaussian kernel $K(\theta;\hat\theta,\sigma_t)$ is exactly the transition kernel of a driftless diffusion wrapped onto the circle,
$f(x,t)\equiv0$ and $g(t)=\sqrt{d\sigma_t^2/dt}$, a variance-exploding schedule in the terminology of \cite{song2021}. The forward process is therefore simply
$$d\theta = g(t)\,dW,$$
and its reverse-time companion, specialized from \S\ref{sec:diffusion-models}'s general form to $f\equiv0$, is
$$d\theta = -g(t)^2\,s_\theta(\theta_t,\sigma_t,\beta)\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar W,$$
so the entire drift of the reverse process is the learned score itself, scaled by $g(t)^2$ and run backward in time; there is no separate deterministic drift term to discretize, unlike in a variance-preserving (DDPM-style) schedule. Starting from
pure noise on the fine lattice, ancestral sampling (the discretized SMLD -- score matching with Langevin dynamics -- update of \cite{song2021}) integrates the
reverse process backward through the noise schedule, with optional Langevin
corrector steps at each level, using the trained score $s_\theta(\theta_t,
\sigma_t,\beta)$.

As the fine configuration is generated, three mechanisms softly bias
$Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ toward $Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$: the coarse-conditioning
channels fed to the network throughout sampling, the FiLM-embedded global
mean described above, and an explicit reconstruction-guidance penalty added
to the score at each noise level, the gradient of
$$-\frac{\big(\sum_{p=1}^4\theta_{p,\mathrm{fine}} - \theta_{p,\mathrm{coarse}}\big)^2}
        {2\lambda(\sigma)}, \qquad \lambda(\sigma)\approx 8\sigma^2,$$
with respect to the cell sum -- $\lambda(\sigma)$ is the noise the eight
boundary links of a coarse cell inject into the blocked plaquette at level
$\sigma$. The resulting per-cell scalar is broadcast to its four fine
sub-plaquettes and assembled into a link-space vector by the same discrete
curl (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:curl-x}--\eqref{eq:curl-y}) used for the score head, so the guidance term is
gauge-covariant by construction rather than by tuning. The residual is
deliberately \emph{not} wrapped: a wrapped residual cannot see a cell sum
that has landed $2\pi$ away from its coarse target, which lets spurious
winding defects freeze in and inflate $\langle Q^2\rangle$ at large $\beta$;
pulling the raw sum to the principal-branch target expels them, at a
computable failure probability (${\sim}0.2\%$ per cell at $\beta=4$,
${\sim}4\times10^{-9}$ at $\beta=14$).

None of these three mechanisms is what makes $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}=Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$
exact, and by the freezing argument of \S\ref{sec:freezing} none of them
\emph{can} be: $Q$ changes only when some plaquette angle is pushed past
$(-\pi,\pi]$, so any gauge-covariant correction assembled by the curl of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:curl-x}--\eqref{eq:curl-y} -- including the reconstruction-guidance
term above -- is a smooth deformation of the link field that leaves $Q$
exactly invariant unless a plaquette actually wraps. These three mechanisms
only make the sampler more likely to settle near the correct sector on its
own; they do not guarantee it.

Exactness instead comes from a fourth, deterministic step applied directly
to the link field rather than to the score: once every ten reverse-SDE
steps after the noise level $\sigma$ drops below $\sigma\approx0.5$ -- the
point at which $Q$ becomes a well-defined quantity of the noisy sample --
and once more, unconditionally, after sampling completes, the sampler
computes $\Delta Q = Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} - Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ on the current
sample and adds $\Delta Q$ copies of the smooth unit-winding instanton field
of \S\ref{sec:instanton} to the link angles, then rewraps. Unlike the
classical instanton update of \S\ref{sec:instanton_updates_classical}, this
is not a Metropolis proposal: there is no accept/reject, the shift is
applied unconditionally, and because adding a unit-winding field changes $Q$
by exactly one by construction, a single application -- iterated up to three
times to catch the rare case where the shift itself pushes a further
plaquette past $(-\pi,\pi]$ and overshoots -- leaves
$Q_{\mathrm{fine}}=Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$ exactly. Applying this projection
partway through the reverse process, rather than only at the end, lets the
remaining diffusion steps and the rethermalization tail relax the resulting
$O(2\pi\Delta Q/V)$ uniform strain (\S\ref{sec:instanton}) instead of leaving
all of it to rethermalization; the final, unconditional application is what
guarantees the delivered configuration's topology regardless of how close
the first three mechanisms got on their own.

\subsubsection{Extension to U(2)}

This carries over to U(2) essentially unchanged. The lifting step of one inverse-RG
move for U(2) is a conditional diffusion model that generates only the
determinant sector $\psi \equiv \arg\det U = \mathrm{wrap}(2\phi)$, with $\psi$ standing in for $\theta$. No new generative model is built for the non-abelian field. The U(1) coupling solving
$$\frac{I_1(\beta_1)}{I_0(\beta_1)} =
\frac{\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos\alpha\;w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta_{U(2)})\,d\alpha}
     {\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta_{U(2)})\,d\alpha},$$
where $\alpha$ is the determinant-sector plaquette angle and
$w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta) = 2I_1(z)/z$, $z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2)$, is its exact
single-plaquette marginal weight, derived by integrating the SU(2) sector out
of the action exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:su2-marginal}). This is the
minimum-KL projection of the determinant-sector marginal onto a U(1) Wilson
family, with $\beta_1 \to \beta_{U(2)}/4$ as $\beta_{U(2)}\to\infty$.

The SU(2) sector needs no model at all. Conditioning on $\phi = \psi/2$, the
U(2) action reduces to the standard heatbath conditional
$p(q \mid \psi) \propto \exp(\beta\, k \cdot q)$ \cite{creutz1980}, where $k$ is the SU(2) staple -- the sum, over the two plaquettes touching a given link, of the product of the other three links in each plaquette, projected into the same quaternion representation as $q$, so a fresh SU(2) sector can
be equilibrated exactly at frozen $\psi$ by Creutz--Kennedy--Pendleton heatbath/overrelaxation sweeps \cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985}, which leave $\psi$, and hence $Q$, unchanged. Appendix~\ref{app:su2-marginal} derives both results from the same starting point: the joint U(2) action does \emph{not} factorize into independent U(1) and SU(2) pieces, and conditioning, rather than independence, is what makes both the heatbath conditional and the determinant-sector marginal exact.

\subsubsection{Applying instanton updates during diffusion sampling}
\label{sec:instanton_apply}

For U(2), the deterministic projection above (the fourth mechanism of
\S\ref{sec:reverse_diffusion}) is applied to $\psi$ exactly as it is to
$\theta$ for U(1): $\Delta Q$ copies of the single-quantum instanton field
are added and rewrapped, unconditionally and with no Metropolis step. This
sidesteps the classical odd/even mobility gap of
\S\ref{sec:instanton_updates_classical} entirely, since that gap is a
property of proposal \emph{acceptance}, and the deterministic shift never
proposes anything to accept or reject: a copy of the odd-changing instanton
field is simply added to $\psi$ whenever $\Delta Q$ is odd, at the same cost
as an even one. The classical baselines pay the mobility gap because they
sample it; the generative route pays nothing, because it transports $Q$
rather than sampling it.

\section{The result: seed quality}
\label{sec:seedquality}

\subsection{Exact values}
\label{sec:exact-values}

2D compact U(1) and 2D U(2) are exactly solvable, so at
large $\beta$, where HMC freezes, the reference (for training) is not another simulation
but a closed form. The observables scored throughout this paper, Wilson
loops up to $12\times12$ (to probe both ultraviolet (UV) and infrared (IR) structure), the
topological charge distribution $P(Q)$, and the topological susceptibility
$\chi_t$, all follow from the single-plaquette character coefficients of
each theory. Table~\ref{tab:u1-exact-observables} (U(1)) and
Table~\ref{tab:u2-exact-observables} (U(2)), given in
Appendix~\ref{app:exact}, collect the closed forms used throughout; U(2)'s
determinant sector additionally admits an exact single-plaquette marginal
weight (derived in this work, Table~\ref{tab:u2-exact-observables}).

\subsection{Observables comparison}

Every comparison in this section is against a closed-form reference. Local observables
and topology are scored separately and never collapsed into one number:
$t_{\rm therm}$ and $\langle Q^2\rangle$ against exact.

\paragraph{Relaxation-time estimator.}
\label{sec:relaxation-correction}
$t_{\rm therm}$ is a fitted exponential relaxation time rather than a discrete
threshold crossing, because a threshold rule (first trajectory where
$|z|\leq2$ holds for five consecutive records) is rugged in coupling --- one
lagging chain among many shifts the crossing substantially --- and unusable
once thermalization is $O(1)$ trajectory, where the integer answer swings any
derived ratio by a physically meaningless factor. We fit
the transient decay of the observable mean toward its exact value,
$\mathrm{mean\_obs}(t)\approx\mathrm{target}+A\,e^{-t/\tau}$, by weighted
nonlinear least squares, following Detmold and Endres
\cite{detmoldendres2015,detmoldendres2016} and adapted to a variable number of
jointly-fit observables. The fitted $\tau$ carries a chain-resampling
significance gate ($\hat\tau/\sigma_{\hat\tau}\geq2$), which is necessary: a
plain delta-$\chi^2$ goodness-of-fit test is fooled by the autocorrelation
already present in a Markov chain and will fit a large, fictitious $\tau$ to
flat noise.

\paragraph{What a relaxation time can and cannot compare.} A fitted $\tau$
answers ``how fast does this chain approach the exact value'', not ``how good
is this seed'', and the two come apart consequentially. A seed far from target
relaxes visibly, so the fit succeeds and returns a finite $\tau$; a seed
\emph{already at} target has no transient to resolve, so the same fit returns
$\tau=\infty$ or is vetoed. Any criterion of the form ``does the seed resolve
a finite relaxation time'' therefore rewards seeds that leave room to
converge, and cannot separate $\tau=0$ (already correct, the best outcome)
from a large finite $\tau$. It is not a seed-quality measure and is not used
as one here.

Where seeds or checkpoints are compared for \emph{quality}, the endpoint is
the raw seed's distance from exact before any trajectory: the relative
deviation and the deviation in units of the across-chain standard error, each
maximised over $\{$plaquette, $W(2\times2)$, $W(4\times4)\}$. It is fit-free.
Both are reported because neither suffices alone --- $z=\sqrt{N}\,\mathrm{bias}/\sigma$,
so a noisier checkpoint scores a smaller $z$ at equal bias, while a relative
deviation ignores that the theory's own spread varies by orders of magnitude
across couplings. $\tau$ is retained for the question it does answer: the
trajectory cost of rethermalizing a given start (\S\ref{sec:cost}).

\paragraph{Statistical methodology.} Every estimator, interval and gate
used in this paper is collected in App.~\ref{app:stats}, including which
quantities deliberately carry no statistical error bar and why. In brief:
each chain's own time series is
first reduced to one per-chain time average, and the reported standard
error is the standard-error-of-the-mean taken across chains. Pooling a Markov chain's own samples instead would
underestimate the error whenever the chain is autocorrelated, and it
would report a vanishing error on a chain that is topologically frozen
even though it has sampled nothing. Resampling whole chains keeps a
frozen chain's error bar honest instead of manufacturing spurious
precision from a long frozen series. Sector agreement uses a
Mahalanobis-distance goodness-of-fit statistic on the per-chain sector-frequency
vectors, with a bootstrapped $p$-value. When many
observable $z$-scores are averaged together (e.g.\ Fig.~\ref{fig:u1_z_dist}),
the appropriate null is not $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ elementwise but
$\mathrm{mean}|z|\to\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.798$ under a half-normal, and the
standard error on that mean must be computed from the effective observable count: Wilson loops of different sizes are
strongly correlated observables of the same underlying field, so treating
them as independent draws systematically overstates significance. We
estimate the effective count from the participation ratio of the
empirical observable-correlation matrix and quote it alongside any
averaged $|z|$.

\subsubsection{U(1) observables comparison}
\label{sec:u1obs}

The central claim being tested here is that the diffusion model produces a
\emph{seed}, not the final configuration: all four arms below -- the
diffusion seed and the cold, hot, and winding-augmented classical starts --
are run through the same HMC chain and compared \emph{after}
thermalization (Fig.~\ref{fig:u1_case_l64} shows the delivered U(1) ensemble
against exact at the hardest coupling tested), i.e.\ once each arm's own observables have settled within
statistical error, not on their raw output. What differs between arms is
how many trajectories that takes, $t_{\rm therm}$, and whether it happens
within the trajectory budget at all. The comparison is the diffusion seed
against a cold and a hot start, under
the classical baseline of record, HMC with the winding update. The seed
sits inside the exact plaquette's error band already at $t=0$, before any
HMC trajectory is run, at every coupling tested, while the classical
baseline of record (cold start, HMC with the winding update) needs a
one-time burn-in of 8--58 seconds to reach the same band at the two easier
couplings tested ($\beta_f=4.44,14.15$), and at the two hardest
($\beta_f=55.02,218.58$) does not cleanly reach it at all within the tested
budget: it still \emph{fails} the quality criterion at 500 trajectories and
is only \emph{marginal} -- not a clean pass -- after 8000
(Table~\ref{tab:u1_h2h}).
The seed's advantage does not close with volume: at fixed
$\beta_f=14.1464$ across a $16\times$ volume range its plaquette bias at
$t=0$ is constant to within 15\%, though $t_{\rm therm}$ itself grows as
the measurement threshold tightens. Full tables and supporting figures are
in App.~\ref{app:u1cov}.

\subsubsection{U(2) observables comparison}
\label{sec:u2obs}

The robust claim is a threshold, not a gradient: past the training
checkpoint's highest coupling the seed fails to thermalize within budget in
$12$ of $14$ tested (coupling, volume, round) combinations. Pooling all four
coverage checkpoints, both volumes and both rounds --- one checkpoint's rungs
span too narrow a range of gaps to resolve this alone --- whether a coupling thermalizes \emph{at all} is dominated by log-distance
to the nearest training rung: mean log-gap $0.90$ for couplings that converge
against $2.83$ for those that never do (Mann--Whitney $p=6.0\times10^{-8}$,
$n=176$). On out-of-sample couplings, scored by the fit-free endpoint of
\S\ref{sec:relaxation-correction}, Spearman(log-gap, $\log Z$) $=+0.63$
($p=2\times10^{-19}$, $n=166$, bootstrap CI $[0.50,0.72]$) --- so
distance-to-nearest-rung is the dominant culprit behind the apparently
erratic scatter in seed quality.

\textbf{This must be read in $Z$, not in relative deviation.} The same
correlation on relative deviation is $-0.16$: across couplings, gap is
confounded with $\beta$ and the theory's own spread falls by orders of
magnitude, so a ratio shrinks for reasons unrelated to the model. Comparisons
\emph{at fixed coupling} (Table~\ref{tab:volume_scaling}) are immune, both
arms facing the identical spread, and there the ratio is the more direct
statement.

\textbf{The gradient across checkpoints is the coverage effect from a third
direction.} \texttt{cov60} gives $\rho=+0.84$ and \texttt{default} $+0.88$
(both $p<10^{-4}$, $n=44,42$), while \texttt{wide} gives $+0.35$ ($p=0.028$)
and \texttt{wide\_dense} $+0.25$ ($p=0.13$, not resolved), $n=40$ each. A
narrow checkpoint's seed quality is strongly governed by distance to its
nearest rung; a wide one's is barely governed by it, which is what having
rungs everywhere buys. Two consequences. A pooled correlation manufactured
entirely by between-checkpoint differences is excluded by the two narrow
checkpoints alone, each decisive on its own data. But it does not hold within
\emph{every} checkpoint --- the densest checkpoint's dependence is not
resolved, which is weak evidence \emph{for} saturation. Weak: at $n=40$, $+0.25$ is not distinguishable from
$+0.35$. Seed quality remains measurably rugged between neighbouring
couplings even after this correction (Table~\ref{tab:u2_coverage}),
consistent with second-order effects --- individual training-rung quality
(\S\ref{sec:coverage-u2}'s coverage-empty rung) and volume --- governing the
residual once gap is controlled for.

Gap also costs more at larger volume: at matched fractional gap the seed's
$t_{\rm therm}$ moves from a handful of trajectories to never converging
within budget one volume apart (Table~\ref{tab:u2_volume}). On the same two-yardstick test
as U(1), applied at both benchmarked couplings, every diffusion-seeded arm
stays within $2.5\sigma$ of exact simultaneously on every Wilson loop size
and on $\langle Q^2\rangle$; no cold- or hot-started arm does both at once.
The best any classical arm manages on topology alone is $0.25$--$0.76\sigma$
(cold start with the marginal odd-charge move), but that same arm is
$1.8$--$39\sigma$ off on the Wilson loops it leaves unsampled; the best any
classical arm manages on the Wilson loops alone is $\sim\!1.1\sigma$ (cold
start with the even-charge move), but that arm's topology is $3$--$7\sigma$
off. A hot start is off by hundreds of $\sigma$ on both
(Tables~\ref{tab:u2_topology}, \ref{tab:u2_observables}). Full tables and
supporting figures are in App.~\ref{app:u2cov}.

\paragraph{Why this is a fixed-budget snapshot, not a fitted relaxation
time.} Every arm above is scored after an \emph{identical} budget ($400$
trajectories, $64$ chains) rather than by its own fitted $t_{\rm therm}$, for
a measured reason. The relaxation estimator assumes the observable mean
approaches its target as a single exponential. That holds for a diffusion
seed, which starts near equilibrium with a small residual, but not for a
classical chain from a cold or hot start, whose approach is visibly
multi-scale. Reanalysing every saved series makes the asymmetry quantitative:
the joint fit is rejected as a poor absolute description for $89.8\%$
$[84.4,93.4]$ of U(2) cold-start arms and $55.7\%$/$68.8\%$ of U(1) cold/hot
arms, against $0.0\%$ $[0.0,2.1]$ and $6.8\%$ for the diffusion seed
(Wilson $95\%$ intervals, $n=176$ arm-observable fits) --- non-overlapping in
every case, and the couplings where both sides do yield a trustworthy $\tau$
all sit at the extreme weak-coupling end. A speed comparison built on fitted
$\tau$ would therefore be unavailable for exactly the couplings the method
targets. The fixed-budget snapshot assumes no functional form and is
well-defined for every arm at every coupling. All eight arms are advanced by
one batched sampler instance per run, so they differ only in their starting
configuration and, where stated, in which winding move is enabled. Where a
fitted $\tau$ \emph{is} quoted --- the coverage and volume analyses, which
score the seed against exact rather than against a classical arm --- it must
pass the three gates of App.~\ref{app:stats}.

\subsection{Cost accounting}
\label{sec:cost}

Cost here means interval time, trajectories needed to reach an
independent, equilibrated configuration. Two different interval-time quantities appear together below and
should not be conflated: the classical arm's entry cost is a
one-time burn-in paid once per chain before any configuration is trusted
(Table~\ref{tab:u1_h2h}), while its steady-state cost is the
much smaller $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$ paid between every pair of independent samples (Table~\ref{tab:table-s8}).


The winding-augmented classical chain needs
$10.1$, $13.5$, and $17.2$ trajectories between independent samples; the
diffusion seed reaches local equilibrium in $0$, $0$, and $6$ trajectories
after the lift. At every tested coupling the seed is already at or below
the classical interval from the point it is delivered.
The same question is sharper here because of the even/marginal mobility gap
established in \S\ref{sec:instanton_updates_classical}: one of the two
classical moves measures the wrong thing. Figure~\ref{fig:u2_2d:fig07_topological_reach} compares plain HMC, the
even-only move, and the marginal move against the diffusion seed directly
in trajectories, rather than in mobility statistics. As in u1, the
diffusion seed inherits its sector from a coupling where sampling is
affordable, so it pays none of this interval-time cost at generation time.

\section{Does Wider Training Coverage Help?}
\label{sec:coverage}

Figs.~\ref{fig:coverage_beta} and \ref{fig:coverage_volume} summarise this
section's result along its two axes. In coupling, a checkpoint's seed stays
usable until roughly its own training ceiling and degrades past it, so the
edge is a property of the checkpoint and not of the coupling. In volume, the
penalty for climbing one rung is itself set by coverage: the narrowest
checkpoint loses an order of magnitude between $L=32$ and $L=64$ while the
widest lose a factor of two. The rest of the section establishes both
quantitatively, theory by theory.

\S\ref{sec:u2obs} established that seed quality falls off past a
checkpoint's training coverage. The natural question is whether simply
training over a wider coupling range fixes this, and at what cost. Both
studies were retrained with the identical architecture and data recipe,
differing only in the coupling range covered, so any quality change is
attributable to coverage alone.

\subsection{U(2)}
\label{sec:coverage-u2}

The \texttt{wide} checkpoint extends training to model $\beta\approx2000$,
against the deployed checkpoint's $\approx104$. All four checkpoints are
scored on the identical coupling grid and by the identical endpoint used for
U(1) (\S\ref{sec:coverage-u1}): $Z=\max|z|$ of the raw diffusion seed at
record $0$, before any trajectory, over $\{$plaquette, $W(2{\times}2)$,
$W(4{\times}4)\}$ against the closed form. Using one endpoint across both
theories is deliberate --- the alternative, scoring U(2) by a relaxation time
and U(1) by seed distance, would make the two studies incomparable on the one
claim they share. Pooled over both volumes and both HMC rounds the
comparison is paired at $n=44$ couplings, of which $30$ lie inside the
deployed checkpoint's training coverage (model $\beta\le104.132$) and $14$
past its ceiling.

\textbf{Past the ceiling the effect is unambiguous and the direction is the
intuitive one.} \texttt{wide} delivers a closer seed than \texttt{default} at
$14/14$ couplings (median $Z$ ratio $9.8\times$, bootstrap CI $[8.6,17.6]$,
Wilcoxon $p=0.0010$), and \texttt{wide\_dense} at $14/14$ ($17.1\times$,
$[15.6,18.4]$, $p=0.0001$). Against the narrowest checkpoint the same
comparisons give $14/14$ at $10.9\times$ and $18.1\times$. In relative
deviation the margins are larger still ($35$--$142\times$), so the conclusion
does not depend on how the deviation is normalised.

\textbf{Inside coverage, wider is not better --- each checkpoint is best where
it was trained.} \texttt{wide} against \texttt{default} is not resolved on the
primary endpoint over the $30$ in-coverage couplings ($17/30$, $p=0.086$),
and the narrowest checkpoint \texttt{cov60} is the \emph{best} of the four
there, beating \texttt{default} at $28/30$ (median ratio $0.3\times$,
$p<10^{-4}$) while losing to it past the ceiling. That pair of facts is the
cleanest single statement of the coverage claim in either theory: quality
tracks where a checkpoint's rungs are, not how many couplings it nominally
spans, and a checkpoint bought its width at some cost to the range it
already had.
Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage_beta} shows the full curve.

\paragraph{Is this a range effect, or a density confound?} \texttt{wide}'s 12
new high-$\beta$ rungs are bare fixed anchor points with no supporting
\texttt{random\_rungs} density in the newly-covered region, unlike the
densely-sampled range \texttt{default}/\texttt{cov60} already cover -- so the
past-ceiling result could in principle be ``sparser sampling hurts'' rather than
``narrower range hurts'' \emph{per se}. \texttt{wide\_dense} retrains the identical
recipe with $31$ additional density-matched rungs (raw $\beta\in[431,1969]$
at $L=8/16/32$, counts scaled to the same log-range density as the existing
\texttt{random\_rungs} block) added on top of \texttt{wide}'s original rungs,
same capacity, evaluated on the identical coupling grid.

\textbf{Density does help, and where the range effect does not.}
\texttt{wide\_dense} beats \texttt{wide} at $35/44$ couplings (median $Z$ ratio
$2.0\times$, CI $[1.4,2.5]$, $p<10^{-4}$). Split at the ceiling, the whole
margin is \emph{inside} coverage --- $27/30$ ($2.0\times$, $p<10^{-4}$)
against $8/14$ past it ($1.7\times$, $p=0.36$). The two knobs therefore act in
different places: range rescues couplings past the old ceiling, where density
does nothing measurable; density improves couplings already within range,
where range does nothing measurable. So the past-ceiling rescue is a genuine
range effect, not an artifact of how sparsely the new rungs were sampled.

Note what this intervention is. \texttt{wide} carries no random rungs at all
--- deliberately, matching the deployed recipe --- so \texttt{wide\_dense}
takes it from $24$ rungs to $55$: a $2.29\times$ enlargement of a training set
with no random coverage whatsoever, and a materially different experiment from
U(1)'s $1.34\times$ addition to an already-dense base
(\S\ref{sec:coverage-u1}).

\textbf{But the gain is not local to the rungs that produced it, and we cannot
explain that.} The $31$ supplement rungs span model $\beta\approx108$--$492$.
Splitting there instead of at \texttt{default}'s ceiling, the entire effect
sits \emph{below} the band --- $27/30$ at $\beta<108$ ($2.04\times$, CI
$[1.36,4.82]$, $p<10^{-4}$) against $8/14$ inside it ($1.72\times$,
$[0.88,2.50]$, $p=0.36$). Adding rungs between $108$ and $492$ improved the
seed below $108$ and did nothing measurable where they actually landed. The
natural reading --- density fills local gaps, the mechanism the
distance-to-nearest-rung correlation describes --- is \emph{not} what these
data show. A generic benefit from more data, or a local gain cancelled inside
the band by capacity dilution, would both produce this, and $n=14$ cannot
separate them.

This null is a different animal from the U(1) density null and must not be
read alike: it is \emph{underpowered}, not bounded. The paired log-ratio
scatters with standard deviation $1.39$ inside the band against $0.44$ below
it --- seed quality at these stiff couplings is genuinely rugged between
neighbouring couplings --- so at $n=14$ the test would detect an effect of the
observed size only $18\%$ of the time; $80\%$ power needs roughly $28$ in-band
couplings against the below-band effect size, $40$ against the observed one.
The U(1) null excludes its comparison effect; this one excludes nothing. We
report the ordering, which is robust, and flag the mechanism and the in-band
magnitude as open.

\paragraph{How much these comparisons can carry.} A paired chain-resampling
bootstrap (App.~\ref{app:stats}) puts the per-coupling $95\%$ interval at
roughly a factor of two, which is why these comparisons are reported as paired
rank tests over the whole grid rather than through named couplings. Such noise
pushes a signed-rank test toward the null, costing power while preserving the
false-positive rate, so the two remaining nulls --- \texttt{wide} against
\texttt{default} inside coverage, \texttt{wide\_dense} against \texttt{wide}
past the ceiling --- bound those effects rather than showing them to be zero.
The positive results are unaffected.

\paragraph{Why a closer training rung can make a coupling worse: a training
rung with no topological coverage.} The null result above hides a real,
checkable mechanism at the one coupling where density-matching moved the
distance-to-nearest-rung the most. In model-$\beta$ space, \texttt{wide}'s
nearest rung to the $L=32$ test point at model $\beta\approx134.3$ sits
$15.65$ away; \texttt{wide\_dense}'s new supplement rung sits $0.19$ away ---
an 82$\times$ improvement, the largest of any tested coupling. Yet the seed
\emph{regresses} there, and by a wide margin on the primary endpoint:
\texttt{wide}'s raw seed sits at $Z=0.45$ and $0.39$ in the two HMC rounds
($215$ and $62$ ppm) --- statistically indistinguishable from exact --- while
the density-supplemented checkpoint sits at $Z=6.31$ and $5.96$ ($672$ and
$562$ ppm), roughly $14\times$ further out. A rung $82\times$ nearer made the
seed an order of magnitude worse. The cause is visible in that supplement rung's own generation record
(raw $\beta=536.629$, $L=16$): \texttt{winding\_acceptance\_rate} $=0.0$, and
the ensemble occupies exactly one topological sector. It was generated by
honest classical sampling with no sector augmentation, and at this coupling
that is the frozen regime of \S\ref{sec:freezing} --- the topological update
never once fired.

\emph{What is and is not wrong with such an ensemble is worth stating
precisely, because the obvious reading is incorrect.} At these couplings the
exact distribution really is almost entirely $Q=0$ --- $P(Q\neq0)$ is
$5\times10^{-4}$ at this rung, and as small as $5\times10^{-9}$ at the
corresponding U(1) couplings --- so a frozen, all-$Q{=}0$ ensemble is
\emph{not} measurably wrong in its sector frequencies. It is correct in
frequency to within one part in $10^{3}$ or better. What it lacks is
\emph{coverage}: not one example of $Q\neq0$ local structure at that
coupling. That is the quantity that matters here, because at deployment the
charge is never drawn from the model --- it is \emph{imposed} by transport
from the coarse ensemble (\S\ref{sec:instanton_apply}), which routinely
carries $Q\neq0$. The network is therefore asked at deployment for
conditional structure at charges it never saw at that coupling, and pulling
the nearest rung closer in $\beta$ simply moved a rung that cannot answer
that question nearer to the test point. Coverage, not frequency, is what
training data has to supply --- the same principle the generalization
argument of \S\ref{sec:conclusions} rests on.

The defect is not confined to one rung or one theory. Measuring the charge
histogram of every training ensemble directly, $33$ of the $41$ U(1) training
rungs above $\beta=200$ occupy a single topological sector, against $5$
sectors each for the $8$ rungs that were sector-augmented; the U(2)
supplement is the same story at $31$ rungs. Any density supplement generated
by plain classical sampling at high $\beta$ has this property, and it is
invisible to every diagnostic that does not look at the sector histogram
itself --- the ensembles pass their plaquette check against the closed form,
because the plaquette is not what is wrong with them.

\emph{The intervention this diagnosis implies was run, and it did not help.}
If missing sector coverage in the supplement is what costs the lift at these
couplings, then supplying it should recover something. Both supplements were
regenerated with instanton augmentation so that every rung spans five sectors
instead of one, and both checkpoints were retrained identically, giving a
clean paired control: the same recipe, the same capacity, the same evaluation
grid, differing only in whether the supplement rungs carry topological
coverage. Neither the training objective nor the delivered seed moved. In
U(1) the final training loss went $0.6125\to0.6098$ and the mean validation
loss over $120$ rungs $1.2332\to1.2359$; scored on the primary endpoint over
the seven couplings both builds share, the retrained checkpoint is
\emph{not} better --- median $Z$ ratio $0.9\times$ (CI $[0.8,1.0]$) and
median relative-deviation ratio $1.0\times$ ($p=0.81$), with the marginal $Z$
signal ($p=0.047$ at $n=7$) pointing the wrong way for the intervention and
bounding any effect at under $20\%$. In U(2), paired over all $44$
coupling/round records, the two builds are indistinguishable on both
normalisations: median ratio $1.0\times$ in each, $p=0.27$ and $p=0.29$. So
the coverage argument above is a mechanism this data is consistent with but
does \emph{not} confirm: the supplement's own sector coverage is not
measurably what makes the difference at these couplings, and the failures it
was invoked to explain remain explained only by distance to the nearest
training rung in coupling.

Stated that way the remedy is immediate, and it is the one the pipeline
already implements elsewhere: sector augmentation
(\S\ref{sec:naive-blocking}) applies random instanton shifts to \emph{already
generated} configurations, so it manufactures the missing coverage without
requiring a sampler that can tunnel at that coupling. Regenerating these
supplement rungs with augmentation enabled raises their per-rung
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ from $0.000$ to $0.79$--$1.03$, i.e.\ from one occupied
sector to several. This is the concrete form of a claim the method needs and
that is easy to state too loosely: \emph{training data can be built at
couplings far above the freezing threshold, because coverage is manufactured
by instanton shifts on existing configurations rather than sampled by a chain
that must tunnel.} The retrained checkpoint's own scan is reported in
\S\ref{sec:coverage-volume}.

\paragraph{A caveat on every number in this subsection.}
\label{sec:per-observable-power}
The joint fit behind $t_{\rm therm}$ shares one $\tau$ across the plaquette,
$W(2\times2)$ and $W(4\times4)$, and refitting each observable with its own
error bar shows why one number can mislead: larger loops carry far more
per-configuration variance, so the same initial deviation is much harder to
resolve on them (signal-to-noise $\approx33$ for the plaquette against
$\approx3.4$ for $W(4\times4)$ at one spot-checked coupling). Accordingly, a
direct test of whether larger loops relax slower finds no effect once each
observable's fit must be independently resolved ($W(4\times4)$ slower in
$2/6$ couplings, $p=0.69$). That does not mean the rates are equal --- it
means $64$ chains cannot tell, and any scale-dependent relaxation claim here
carries that limit.

\subsection{U(1)}
\label{sec:coverage-u1}
wide250 (beta$_{\max}=250$, against the deployed checkpoint's $60$) already
demonstrates the qualitative effect directly: it thermalizes at
$\beta_f=218.58$, a coupling at which classical HMC (with the winding
update) never finishes within budget, where the deployed checkpoint has no
training coverage at all (Fig.~\ref{fig:u1_coverage_comparison}).

wide2000's new coverage (fine $\beta$ up to $2000$) is at $L=16$ only, so a
like-for-like comparison needs matched $L{=}8\to16$ cases run identically
except for the checkpoint. Fifteen couplings ($\beta_f = 250$ to $2600$, all
past deployed's $\beta_{\max}=60$ ceiling by $4$--$43\times$) were run for
three checkpoints of identical architecture and budget ($324{,}954$
parameters, $100$ epochs, the same $78$ randomly-placed low-$\beta$ rungs
apiece, so the fixed coverage rungs are the only difference):
\texttt{deployed} ($\beta_{\max}=60$), \texttt{wide2000} ($\beta_{\max}=2000$,
seven bare anchor rungs), and \texttt{wide2000\_dense} (plus $30$ density
rungs at fine $\beta\in[258,1935]$).

The endpoint is the raw seed's distance from exact, not a relaxation time
(\S\ref{sec:relaxation-correction}): $Z=\max|z|$ over $\{$plaquette,
$W(2{\times}2)$, $W(4{\times}4)\}$ at record $0$, before any trajectory,
against the closed form. Seven of the fifteen couplings were run first and the
endpoint was chosen after inspecting them, so they are not independent
confirmation. The other eight were placed by inverting the ladder relation to
fall in \emph{gaps} between \texttt{wide2000}'s training rungs --- the honest
test of coverage rather than of landing on a rung --- and the analysis was
pre-registered in full before any of them was scored.

\textbf{Both wide checkpoints beat \texttt{deployed} at every coupling.} Over
all fifteen, \texttt{wide2000} wins $15/15$ (median $Z$ ratio $9.4\times$, CI
$[6.9,21.9]$, $p=1\times10^{-4}$) and \texttt{wide2000\_dense} $15/15$
($8.7\times$, $[7.6,18.1]$, $p=1\times10^{-4}$). On the eight off-rung
couplings alone: $8/8$ for both, $8.1\times$ and $8.2\times$, $p=0.0078$ ---
the smallest value a two-sided signed-rank test can return at $n=8$, so the
test is saturated rather than marginal. Relative deviation gives $45\times$
and $50\times$, so nothing turns on the normalisation. Neither pre-registered
falsification criterion fired.

\textbf{Density buys nothing here, and that is a bound rather than a shrug.}
\texttt{wide2000\_dense} against \texttt{wide2000} is $6/15$ (median ratio
$0.94\times$, CI $[0.8,1.2]$, $p=0.80$) and $4/8$ off-rung ($p=0.95$). The
comparison is well aimed: the two share an identical low-$\beta$
\texttt{random\_rungs} block and differ only in the $30$ supplement rungs, and
all fifteen evaluation couplings lie in that span. Nor is it underpowered ---
resampling the observed paired log-ratios and displacing them by a
hypothesised effect, the test detects $2.0\times$ with probability $1.00$,
$1.5\times$ with $0.85$, $1.2\times$ with $0.59$. So U(1) excludes a density
effect of the size U(2) shows and bounds any real effect to $\pm20\%$; only
the small-effect regime stays open.

\textbf{The two theories' density experiments are not the same experiment.}
The intervention is $30$ or $31$ rungs in both, but the base differs:
\texttt{wide2000} already carries $78$ random rungs, so its density arm goes
$89\to119$ ($1.34\times$), while U(2)'s \texttt{wide} carries none and goes
$24\to55$ ($2.29\times$). Both observed differences follow. The \emph{effect}
is larger in U(2) because the perturbation is larger and the base sparser. The
\emph{precision} is larger in U(1) because its arms share $89$ of $119$ rungs
and are near-replicates, so their errors correlate and the paired ratio is
steady --- standard deviation of the paired $\log$ ratio $0.44$ in U(1)
against $1.18$--$1.39$ in U(2). Paired precision is set by how similar the two
arms are, not by how many couplings are scored, which is why fifteen U(1)
couplings resolve more sharply than forty-four U(2) ones. U(1) therefore does
not fail to reproduce U(2)'s density result; it never tested U(2)'s
intervention.

\paragraph{One method, two data recipes.} U(1)'s checkpoints carry $78$ random
rungs; U(2)'s carry none, its \texttt{wide} arm having matched the deployed
recipe so the comparison isolated coverage using data already in hand. This
compromises no claim here, since none differences a U(1) number against a
U(2) one: every comparison is internal to its theory and controlled within it
(identical architecture --- $324{,}954$ parameters in U(1), $403{,}394$ in
U(2) --- identical epochs, identical random-rung block, differing only in the
fixed coverage rungs). That the same conclusion survives two different data
recipes is stronger evidence than one recipe replicated twice.

What it does cost is a single recipe for the reader, so we state the
prescription directly. \emph{What must be covered is the coupling.} Placement
is second order --- U(2) reaches every result here with no random rungs at all
--- and the return on density saturates: going from no random coverage to some
is worth a factor of two (U(2), \S\ref{sec:coverage-u2}), while adding more to
an already-dense range is bounded at $\pm20\%$ (above). Spend a rung budget on
\emph{reaching} the couplings the ladder will visit, not on resampling ones
already reached. We do not claim either theory's pipeline outperforms the
other's: U(1)'s fifteen couplings all sit past its ceiling and were chosen to
be hard, U(2)'s forty-four span both sides of its own, so the two median $Z$
values describe different populations at different volumes in different
theories.

\subsection{Ladder length, and where the error enters}
\label{sec:multi-lift}

Reaching a target $(L,\beta)$ from a base coupling where topology is
genuinely ergodic may take several lifts, so the cost of the rung count
itself has to be measured rather than assumed. Eight cells were run: two
theories $\times$ two endpoints (one inside the checkpoint's training
coverage, one past its ceiling) $\times$ intermediate rethermalization on or
off. Within a cell the endpoint is \emph{fixed} and reached by $1$, $2$ and
$3$ successive lifts from progressively coarser starting ensembles, so the
arms differ only in how many times the model was applied
(Fig.~\ref{fig:multi_lift}).

\paragraph{The rung count is free.} Comparing $|z|$ of the raw lift at the
endpoint, three lifts against one: $1.00\times$ and $0.98\times$ for U(1) at
the in-coverage and past-ceiling endpoints, $0.94\times$ and $1.02\times$
for U(2). With no rethermalization between rungs the same holds
($0.84$--$1.00\times$). Error does not compound along the chain, so a ladder
may be made as long as the base coupling requires.

\paragraph{The error is injected by the final lift.} The per-rung trace of
the U(2) three-lift chain makes this explicit: $z = +15.80$ at $L=16$ (model
$\beta=4.4$), $+0.91$ at $L=32$ ($15.8$), and $-157.44$ at $L=64$ ($61.7$).
The intermediate rungs sit deep inside coverage and land within a few sigma;
the endpoint does not. Endpoint accuracy is therefore set by the final
rung's distance from training coverage, not by the number of rungs climbed,
and the same shape appears in all four chains. This carries a negative
result worth stating plainly: \emph{laddering does not extend the coupling
reach}. Each lift multiplies $\beta$ by about four, so the last lift lands
at the same fine coupling whatever path reached it, and that rung's coverage
is what binds. What the ladder buys is volume at fixed coupling coverage,
which is consistent with \S\ref{sec:coverage-volume} below.

\paragraph{Transport survives composition, and the tail re-samples.} With no
rethermalization between rungs, $100\%$ of configurations still carry their
starting topological charge after $1$, $2$ and $3$ lifts, in both theories
and at both endpoints --- the single-lift transport identity holds under
composition, configuration by configuration. Turning the ladder's own
intermediate rethermalization back on, the fraction kept tracks how weakly
coupled the intermediate rung is: U(1) keeps $33.6\%$ and $81.2\%$
(rethermalizing at $L=16$ at $\beta = 3.87$ and $5.24$, where instantons are
cheap and local heatbath moves change $Q$ freely), U(2) keeps $98.4\%$ and
$100\%$ at its far stiffer intermediate rungs. This is re-sampling rather
than corruption, and the direction shows it: on the U(1) chain
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ moves from $1.633$ to $1.539$ against an exact $1.386$,
i.e.\ \emph{toward} the exact value, because a rung weak enough for local
moves to change $Q$ is a rung where those moves sample $Q$ correctly. The
framing has to be stated accordingly: as deployed, the ladder re-samples
topology at every rung where that remains valid and transports it unchanged
once the coupling is stiff enough that it does not. Only with intermediate
rethermalization off is the charge literally drawn once at the base and
carried unchanged to the top.

\paragraph{Wider coverage improves the raw lift where it does not improve
the delivered one.} Rerunning the two U(2) chains with \texttt{wide\_dense}
in place of \texttt{default}, at identical endpoints, shrinks the raw
endpoint error by an order of magnitude: $|z(W(1\times1))| = 167.4 \to 20.9$
at the in-coverage endpoint and $244.5 \to 15.7$ past the ceiling. Yet after
the rethermalization tail both checkpoints deliver $|z| \lesssim 2.6$ at
every loop size, and the sign tests of \S\ref{sec:coverage-u2} --- which
score the \emph{delivered} ensemble --- accordingly find no in-coverage
advantage for wider coverage. Both statements are true and they are not in
tension: cheap local sweeps repair ultraviolet error, so a large improvement
in the raw seed can be invisible in the product. It matters anyway wherever
the raw seed is the product, which is precisely the regime this method is
aimed at --- and it is a reminder that a coverage comparison scored only
after the tail understates what coverage buys.

\subsection{What the coverage has to cover: coupling, not volume}
\label{sec:coverage-volume}

The score network is fully convolutional and conditioned on the matched
coupling $\beta_{\rm model}$ (\S\ref{sec:scoring-network}), never on lattice
size, so nothing in the architecture ties it to the volumes it saw. That is an
argument, not a measurement, and the ladder confounds the two: each inverse-RG
step doubles $L$ and quadruples $\beta$ together, so a checkpoint failing at a
larger volume is always simultaneously being asked about a stronger coupling.
The retrained checkpoints of \S\ref{sec:coverage-u2} separate them, differing
\emph{only} in coupling range and not at all in training volumes.

The setup makes this clean. No U(2) checkpoint here trains on any $L=64$ data:
\texttt{default} and \texttt{wide} each carry one $L=64$ rung flagged
reference-only and excluded from training (it is what the top ladder rung is
scored against, so training on it would make that column circular), and
\texttt{cov60} has none. The largest training volume is $L=32$. Moreover all
$12$ of \texttt{wide}'s new rungs (raw $\beta=600$ to $2000$) sit at $L=8$,
the \emph{smallest} volume in the recipe. So if widening coverage at $L=8$

coupling coverage transferring across volume.

reproduces the pattern on its own data and also beats \texttt{wide} at both

above is measured along the ladder, where volume and coupling move together.
U(1) supplies the complementary experiment: the same seeded-versus-fresh
comparison at \emph{fixed} $\beta_f = 14.1464$ and $L = 32$, $64$, $128$, so
volume is the only variable (Fig.~\ref{fig:u1_volume_scan}). Scored with the same joint relaxation-time
estimator used for U(2) (App.~\ref{app:stats}; the estimator was ported into
the U(1) package so the two studies are not evaluated by different rules),
the seed sits at its plateau immediately at $L=32$ and $L=64$ ($\tau = 0$,
$\chi^2/\mathrm{dof} = 1.28$ and $2.04$) and resolves a genuine decay of
$\tau = 30.1 \pm 7.2$ trajectories at $L=128$ ($\chi^2/\mathrm{dof} =
1.03$). Against the standard chain's own decorrelation interval at each
volume ($8.7$, $35.0$, $64.1$ trajectories) the seed's entry cost stays
below the interval at every volume, with margin $8.7$, $35.0$ and $34.0$
trajectories.

The sharper statement comes from the fit quality rather than the fitted
times. The diffusion seed is the \emph{only} arm at any volume that the
relaxing-exponential model describes at all: its $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ of
$1.03$--$2.04$ sits inside the $0.6$--$2.1$ range Detmold and Endres report
as healthy for this class of fit, while every fresh cold or hot start
returns $13$, $33$, $81$, $83$, $624$, $684$ --- values at which the model
is rejected outright, because those chains are not decaying toward the exact
value within the window at all. So at fixed coupling the seed's advantage
does not merely shrink slowly with volume; the classical arms remain in a
qualitatively different state at all three volumes, and the growth of the
seed's own $\tau$ from $0$ to $30$ is tracked by a decorrelation interval
growing from $8.7$ to $64.1$ over the same range.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusions}
Full QCD --- or even pure 4D SU(3) --- differs from the theories studied here
in three respects relevant to this method: it is not exactly solvable, it has
no trivial blocking map, and it has no trivial instanton update. We take each
in turn.

\paragraph{Exact solvability is a convenience, not a requirement.} The
closed-form $P(Q)$ is used here only to choose sector frequencies in the
training data; at deployment $Q$ is imposed entirely by transport. Coverage
could instead be manufactured by applying instanton updates to existing
configurations. The training data already takes a step in that direction: the
sector augmentation of \S\ref{sec:naive-blocking} shifts a fixed fraction of
high-$\beta$ configurations to a charge drawn \emph{uniformly} at random,
independent of the true $P(Q)$, so the network sees nonzero-$Q$ local
structure that honest HMC would leave unrepresented once $P(Q)$ collapses onto
$Q=0$. This suggests that matching sector \emph{frequencies} is unnecessary
and only sector \emph{coverage} matters, since transport rather than the
network fixes $Q$. A partial controlled test supports it: regenerating both
theories' high-$\beta$ supplements so every rung spans five sectors instead of
one, and retraining identically, moves neither the training objective nor the
delivered seed (\S\ref{sec:coverage-u2}). That varies coverage in the
supplement rungs only, so it bounds rather than isolates the effect.

On that reading, the extension to a theory where $P(Q)$ is unsolvable is to
manufacture sector coverage by any topology-changing mechanism applied to
existing configurations --- an approximate instanton, or a
parallel-tempering-on-boundary-conditions ladder~\cite{hasenbusch2017} used
purely to visit sectors during training-data construction --- without
requiring that mechanism's acceptance rate or resulting frequencies to be
correct. This is not free-standing: it depends on transport, not the model,
supplying $Q$ at deployment, so it needs an analogous exact (or at least
unbiased) transport step to exist in the target theory. For 4D SU(3) that is
a separate open question, not one coverage augmentation resolves.

\paragraph{The blocking map and the instanton update.} The naive blocking map
used here to pair fine and coarse configurations while exactly preserving $Q$
does not generalize to 4D SU(3). Nor does the instanton update: it was
available for U(2) because U(2) decomposes into a U(1) sector and a
topologically trivial SU(2) one, with the charge carried entirely by the
abelian piece, so a U(1) construction generalizes directly. SU(3) admits no
such decomposition. Finding an approximately exact instanton update for SU(3)
is therefore a central open question for this method.

\paragraph{The architectural point.} Generating UV structure and
generating or preserving IR and topological structure are different jobs, each
best done by the tool suited to it: HMC for the UV, a learned generative model
for the IR and topological content HMC cannot reach on its own. Normalizing
flows \cite{albergo2019}, gauge-equivariant L-CNNs \cite{favoni2020} and other
diffusion architectures \cite{zhu2024,zhu2025,aarts2026} are natural
candidates for extending this division of labor to more complicated theories.

\section{The blocking identity}
\label{app:blocking_proof}

The coarse plaquette angle produced by the naive blocking map of
\S\ref{sec:naive-blocking} equals the wrapped sum of its four constituent fine
plaquette angles exactly, which is what forces
$Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}=Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$.

Sum the four fine plaquette angles (Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-plaquette}) tiling one
$2\times2$ block. Every link interior to the block borders two of the four
sub-plaquettes with opposite orientation, so those contributions cancel
pairwise, leaving the eight boundary links grouped in two collinear pairs per
side. Each collinear pair is exactly what the blocking map sums into one
coarse link, so substituting the coarse links into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:theta-plaquette} reproduces the same eight-term sum. Wrapping to
$(-\pi,\pi]$ commutes with the identity up to $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ on each side,
which leaves $Q$ unchanged.

\section{The U(2) conditional and marginal weights}
\label{app:su2-marginal}

Two exact results are used in \S\ref{sec:instanton_updates_classical} and
\S\ref{sec:method}. Both follow from one fact: the joint action does
\emph{not} factorize into independent U(1) and SU(2) pieces.

Writing $U=e^{i\phi}q$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:u2-link}), the plaquette density is a
product rather than a sum of its sectors,
$\tfrac12\mathrm{Re\,Tr}(U_p) = \cos(\omega_p)\cos(\phi_p)$ with
$q_{0,p}=\cos(\omega_p)$. Expanding,
$\cos\omega_p\cos\phi_p = 1-\phi_p^2/2-\omega_p^2/2+\phi_p^2\omega_p^2/4+\dots$:
the sectors decouple only at Gaussian order, so generating $\psi$ and $q$
independently is wrong at $O(\phi^2\omega^2)$. Sampling them
\emph{conditionally}, $p(\psi,q)=p(\psi)\,p(q\mid\psi)$, is exact and costs
nothing extra, because both factors are available in closed form.

\paragraph{Conditional SU(2).} At frozen $\phi$ the local weight in $q$ is
exactly $\exp(\beta\,\mathbf{k}\cdot q)$ with $\mathbf{k}$ built from the
link's staple sum, which is the standard SU(2) single-link form. It is
therefore sampled exactly by Creutz--Kennedy--Pendleton heatbath
\cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985}, and those sweeps leave $\phi$ --- and
hence $\psi$ and $Q$ --- bit-for-bit unchanged.

\paragraph{Marginal determinant sector.} Integrating SU(2) out of one
plaquette against normalized Haar measure is the same standard one-link
integral \cite{creutz1980},
\begin{equation}
    \int dq\;\exp\big(\beta\,q_{0,p}\cos\phi_p\big) = \frac{2I_1(z)}{z},
    \qquad z=\beta\cos\phi_p ,
    \label{eq:su2-marginal-integral}
\end{equation}
so with $\alpha=2\phi_p$ the marginal weight of $\psi$ is
\begin{equation}
    w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta) = \frac{2I_1(z)}{z}, \qquad z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2).
    \label{eq:wdet}
\end{equation}
At large $\beta$, using $I_1(z)\sim e^{z}/\sqrt{2\pi z}$,
\begin{equation}
    -\log w_{\det} = \mathrm{const} + \beta\big[1-\cos(\alpha/2)\big]
      + \tfrac32\log\cos(\alpha/2) + O(\alpha^4).
\end{equation}
Matching the quadratic term to the U(1) Wilson form $(\beta_1/2)\alpha^2$ gives
the tree-level relation $\beta_1=\beta/4$ used as a leading-order guide
throughout. The $\tfrac32\log\cos(\alpha/2)$ measure term is why $\beta/4$ is
only leading order, and why anything needing an analytic U(1) coupling uses the
minimum-KL projection of Table~\ref{tab:u2-exact-observables} instead.

\section{Statistical methodology}
\label{app:stats}

This appendix consolidates every estimator and interval used in the paper,
so that what each reported comparison is actually comparing can be checked
rather than inferred.

\paragraph{Resampling unit.} Every error bar on an ensemble quantity
resamples \emph{chains}, not configurations within a chain. Pooling a
Markov chain's own samples underestimates the error whenever the chain is
autocorrelated, and in the pathological case reports a vanishing error on a
topologically frozen chain that has in fact sampled nothing. Where an
ensemble is generated rather than sampled -- a diffusion lift, whose
configurations descend from independent base chains -- configurations are
themselves the independent unit, and a config-level bootstrap is used; for
those ensembles the bootstrap and the naive standard error of the mean
agree to within a few percent, which we verify rather than assume.

\paragraph{Relaxation time $t_{\rm therm}$, and its three gates.} $t_{\rm
therm}$ is a fitted exponential relaxation time
(\S\ref{sec:relaxation-correction}): observables are fit jointly, sharing a
single $\tau$, following Detmold and Endres
\cite{detmoldendres2015,detmoldendres2016}. A reported $\tau$ must clear
three independent gates. (i) A delta-$\chi^2$ test against the flat null,
at the Wilks threshold for the number of extra parameters, establishes that
a decay is resolved at all. (ii) A chain-resampling-bootstrap significance
gate, $\hat\tau/\sigma_{\hat\tau}\geq2$, rejects a decay that is unstable
under resampling which chains contribute; this is necessary because a
delta-$\chi^2$ test alone is fooled by the autocorrelation intrinsic to a
Markov chain's time series and will fit a large fictitious $\tau$ to what
is actually flat noise. (iii) An absolute goodness-of-fit veto on
$\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ rejects a fit that clears both of the preceding
\emph{relative} tests while still describing the data badly in absolute
terms. Gate (iii) is not hypothetical: classical arms routinely produce
formally-significant fits at $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ of $40$--$9000$ against
the $0.6$--$2.1$ range Detmold and Endres report for genuine fits. The veto
is applied to $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ directly rather than to a formal
$p$-value, because a $p$-value test grows arbitrarily strict as the degrees
of freedom grow (several hundred here) and would reject healthy fits purely
from statistical power. A fit failing gate (iii) is reported as
\emph{unresolved}, distinct from both ``already at target'' ($\tau=0$) and
``never converges'' ($\tau=\infty$); the latter is established by a
separate bootstrap test that the settled tail is biased away from target.


\paragraph{The three gates, and where their thresholds come from.} Two need
no tuning. Whether the exponential improves on a flat line at the exact value
is tested by the change in $\chi^2$ against Wilks' critical value at $95\%$
for the number of extra parameters, which generalises correctly as more
observables are fit jointly; requiring the fitted $\tau$ to exceed twice its
chain-resampling bootstrap error is the ordinary two-standard-error
convention, and it works because a spurious drift in autocorrelated noise is
unstable under resampling which chains contribute while a genuine decay is
not.

The third, the absolute $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ veto, is the one arbitrary
choice, and it decides whether a classical arm reports a finite relaxation
time at all, so we calibrated it. A nominal $\chi^2$ tail probability will not
serve: successive records are correlated in simulation time, so although
$E[\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}]=1$ still holds under a correct model, its
\emph{variance} exceeds the nominal $2/\mathrm{dof}$ and with several hundred
degrees of freedom a nominal $p$-value becomes arbitrarily strict. Calibrating
instead against a synthetic null where the model is true by construction
(exponential decay plus AR(1) noise, independent across chains) over $1600$
replicas spanning $\rho=0$ to $0.9$, the null $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ reaches a
maximum of $1.68$ and the false-rejection rate is $0/1600$ at the deployed
threshold of $5$.

The measured separation makes the exact value immaterial. The $121$ resolved
diffusion-seed fits span $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}=0.07$--$2.8$ (median $1.07$,
consistent with the $0.6$--$2.1$ Detmold and Endres report for healthy fits),
while the $322$ classical-arm fits span $3.26$--$9108$ (median $85$). The
populations do not overlap, so every threshold in $[2.8,3.26]$ gives identical
classifications and no conclusion depends on where in that gap it sits.

A chain-resampling bootstrap on the settled tail, which needs no threshold,
answers a \emph{different} question and is not a substitute: it flags $52\%$
of seed fits, because a chain still relaxing when the window ends has a biased
tail while its exponential description remains excellent. Model adequacy and
completed convergence are distinct; the veto tests the first, and the tail test
only separates ``already at target'' from ``stuck away from target'' where no
decay is resolved.

Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}, separating those taken from the literature, those
that are standard conventions, and those this
work chose. Two of the last category were removed or derived during a
methodology audit, leaving one, which is calibrated below.

Two entries deserve their own note because they were changed by the audit
rather than merely documented. The Wilson-loop criterion of $|z|\le2.5$, used
throughout as the agreement test, was adopted before it was derived; it is in
fact the multiplicity-corrected two-sided $5\%$ threshold for the number of
\emph{independent} observables actually being tested. Using the raw count of
$41$ scored observables would give $z=3.23$ and is wrong, because 2D Wilson
loops of different sizes are near-deterministic functions of one another; at
the measured effective count $N_{\rm eff}=3.77$ the \v{S}id\'ak threshold is
$z=2.47$ and at $L=64$'s $N_{\rm eff}=3.25$ it is $2.42$. The deployed $2.5$
is within $0.03$ of both. Separately, the rule deciding whether the cold chain
may supply the decorrelation interval carried an invented factor
($\tau_{\rm cold} < 0.5\,n_{\rm traj}$). It was a crude proxy for
``equilibrated with room to spare'', a question the goodness-of-fit gate now
answers directly, and it was verified redundant --- removing it changes the
interval source on $0$ of $176$ records --- before being deleted.

\paragraph{Why the remaining threshold is not replaced by a bootstrap, having
tried.} The natural way to eliminate the last invented number is to replace it
with a resampling test judged at a conventional significance level, and we
implemented one: fit the model, form per-chain residuals about the fitted
curve, resample whole chains of residuals (which preserves each chain's
autocorrelation in simulation time exactly, without modelling it), add them
back to the fitted curve, refit, and read a $p$-value off the resulting null.
\emph{It does not work, and the reason is worth recording.} The test rejects
$0$ of $121$ diffusion-seed fits and $1$ of $322$ classical fits, against
$307$ for the veto --- it is blind to precisely the failure it was built to
catch. The misspecification here is a plateau offset \emph{common to every
chain}, so resampling chains reproduces it in every replica: the bootstrap
null is centred on the very discrepancy under test. The $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$
statistic avoids this because its noise scale is the across-chain standard
error, which is small precisely when the chains agree with one another, and
therefore does not absorb a common-mode systematic. A resampling scheme is
only as good as the null it imposes, and chain resampling cannot impose a null
that removes a common-mode bias.

\paragraph{Ratios of two fitted quantities.} Cost-efficiency
($\mathrm{interval}/t_{\rm therm}$, with $\mathrm{interval}=2\tau_{\rm
int}$) and speedup are ratios whose numerator and denominator are both
estimated \emph{from the same chains}. They are therefore correlated, and a
ratio whose denominator may approach zero is heavy-tailed and asymmetric,
so independent Gaussian error propagation is wrong on two separate counts.
We instead use a \emph{paired} chain-resampling bootstrap: one resample of
chains per replicate, both ingredients recomputed on that same resample,
the ratio formed, and percentiles taken over replicates. The fraction of
replicates falling into the degenerate branches (seed never converges, so
efficiency is zero) is reported alongside the interval rather than being
absorbed into a symmetric error bar. The choice of interval source (cold
chain where it genuinely equilibrated, seeded chain otherwise) is held
fixed at the full-data selection, so every replicate estimates the same
quantity.

\paragraph{Proportions.} Rates over a finite number of fits -- for
instance the fraction of arms whose relaxation fit fails gate (iii) -- are
reported with Wilson score intervals rather than as bare percentages.

\paragraph{Averaged $|z|$ and correlated observables.} When many observable
$z$-scores are averaged, the null is not elementwise $z\sim\mathcal
N(0,1)$ but $\mathrm{mean}|z|\to\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx0.798$ under a
half-normal. Wilson loops of different sizes are strongly correlated probes
of the same field, so treating them as independent draws overstates
significance; the standard error on an averaged $|z|$ is computed from an
effective observable count estimated by the participation ratio of the
empirical observable-correlation matrix, and that count is quoted alongside
any averaged $|z|$.

\paragraph{Sector agreement.} Topological sector agreement uses a
Mahalanobis-distance goodness-of-fit statistic on per-chain
sector-frequency vectors with a bootstrapped $p$-value, with one bin
dropped to remove the exact multinomial redundancy (frequencies sum to a
constant, so the all-ones direction carries no variance and inverting it
manufactures spurious significance). A charge-conjugation check --
$P(Q)$ is exactly even, so $\mathrm{mean}(\mathrm{sign}\,Q)$ must vanish --
is reported as a test requiring no closed form, and therefore not
attributable to any error in the reference.

\paragraph{What is \emph{not} given a statistical error bar, and what is
given a measured range instead.} Wall-clock ratios (for example the
same-endpoint cost ratio between two classical arms) are timing
measurements. Their uncertainty is machine scheduling jitter, not sampling
noise, and it cannot be recovered from the physics data by any resampling
scheme --- no bootstrap over configurations or chains says anything about
how long a kernel took. The honest alternative is to measure the same work
repeatedly on an otherwise idle device and report the observed spread, which
is what we do: three independent repeats of each timed arm, on a GPU with no
other job running, give a cost ratio of $3.2$ with a range of $2.9$--$3.3$
over the nine repeat pairings. Ratios are therefore quoted to one
significant figure with that measured range, and identified as timing
measurements rather than statistical estimates. Two conditions matter and
are enforced when collecting them: the repeats must run with the device
otherwise idle, since timing under contention measures the contention (the
same scans slowed by $1.25$--$1.34\times$ when three ran concurrently), and
each repeat must write to a fresh output directory, because the benchmark
caches completed arms and would otherwise report the first repeat's numbers
three times.

\paragraph{What the veto rejects, and why that is the informative part.}
Every scan saves its raw per-trajectory series, so the whole coupling/volume
matrix in both studies is scored by refitting under one estimator rather
than by re-running any Monte Carlo. Applying the veto uniformly leaves the
diffusion seed's fitted relaxation times essentially untouched --- they are
bit-identical at every $L=64$ U(2) coupling --- and falls almost entirely on
the \emph{classical} arms. Without it, a cold start is credited with a
finite relaxation time of $\tau\approx2.2$--$2.6$ trajectories at couplings
up to $\beta=1660$, at $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ between $48$ and $243$. Those
fits are rejected, and they should be: a cold start does not thermalize in
two trajectories at $\beta=1660$, and an estimator without an absolute
goodness-of-fit gate reports that it does, because the $\Delta\chi^2$ and
bootstrap tests both ask whether an exponential beats a flat line, not
whether it describes the data. The sharper statement this licenses is that
at $L=64$ \emph{every} cold-start arm is rejected: the right description of
those chains is not that they relax slowly but that they are not relaxing
toward the exact value within the window at all.

\paragraph{The classical arms are not slow; the model does not fit them.}
Applying the same goodness-of-fit veto uniformly across every saved series in
both studies turns the seed-versus-classical comparison into a statement
about \emph{which arm the relaxing-exponential model describes at all}, which
is sharper than any ratio of fitted times. Over the $176$ U(1) records, the
diffusion seed yields a usable fit in $93\%$ of cases ($126$ finite, $38$
never-converged, $12$ rejected), while the cold start is rejected in $56\%$
and the hot start in $69\%$. The U(2) matrix behaves the same way: at $L=64$
\emph{every} cold-start arm is rejected, at $\chi^2/\mathrm{dof}$ between
$48$ and $243$ against the $0.6$--$2.1$ range quoted as healthy for this
class of fit. So the honest description of a classical start at these
couplings is not that it relaxes slowly but that it is not relaxing toward
the exact value within the window at all --- and the corresponding fitted
$\tau$ values in the literature-standard estimator should be read as
undefined rather than large. That the seed is the arm the model \emph{does}
describe, in both theories and at every volume tested, is the cleanest form
of the comparison this paper makes.
