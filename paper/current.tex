\begin{abstract}
Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling of lattice gauge fields suffers from critical
slowing down, and its topological charge can freeze entirely at small lattice
spacing. We use a diffusion model as a preconditioner rather than as a sampler:
it performs one inverse renormalization-group step, upsampling a coarse
configuration to a fine one, and HMC is started from that output and remains the
only sampler. The topological charge is transported from the coarse lattice rather than
generated, because a chain at these couplings stays in whatever sector it is
given for any practical budget of HMC. The network is fully convolutional and conditioned on the
coupling rather than on the lattice size, so a model trained only on lattices up
to $32\times32$ is applied unchanged at $128\times128$. We test the construction in 2D U(1) and U(2). In U(2) it
delivers configurations at $\beta\approx1660$ on a $128\times128$ lattice, where the lifted configuration starts $35$ parts per million from the exact
plaquette against a cold start's $1200$, and after $400$ HMC trajectories
reaches $6$ parts per million against $25$.
At the benchmark coupling a cold chain under plain HMC does not change
topological sector at all, while every preconditioned ensemble reproduces the
exact charge variance to within $15\%$. Counted in trajectories per independent
configuration, the method is break-even against winding HMC below $\beta\approx90$, and cheaper by a median factor of
at least nine above it, where that sampler ceases to equilibrate. Widening the
trained coupling range raises the coupling the method reaches, although range
alone does not guarantee it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Lattice quantum chromodynamics (QCD) provides a nonperturbative formulation of the strong interaction in discretized Euclidean spacetime. In lattice calculations, path integrals are evaluated as ensemble averages over field configurations distributed according to $p(\phi)\propto e^{-S[\phi]}$, where $S$ is the lattice action. These configurations are typically generated using Markov-chain Monte Carlo methods, most commonly Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC).

Calculations at a finite lattice spacing $a$ are affected by discretization effects, and controlled predictions for continuum QCD require simulations at progressively smaller lattice spacings, followed by a continuum extrapolation toward $a\to0$. This continuum limit is computationally challenging. As the lattice spacing decreases, autocorrelation times grow rapidly because of critical slowing down~\cite{schaefer2011}. The problem is particularly severe for topological observables: the barriers separating topological sectors become increasingly difficult for a continuous Markov chain to cross, leading to topological freezing~\cite{luscher2011}. Over a finite simulation, the chain may then explore only a restricted set of topological sectors and fail to represent the target topological-charge distribution. This also makes initialization increasingly consequential: conventional cold or hot starts of HMC contain no information about the target long-distance or topological structure, so the chain must first thermalize, evolving until its configurations are
distributed according to $e^{-S}$, before they can be used for measurements. When topological tunneling is strongly suppressed, correcting an atypical initial sector population can itself become prohibitively
slow.

In this work, we use a diffusion model as a coarse-to-fine preconditioner for HMC. The model learns to invert a single renormalization-group (RG) step: conditioned on a configuration from a matched coarse lattice, it generates a configuration on the corresponding finer lattice. The coarse configuration provides the long-distance and topological information, while the learned
inverse map restores the short-distance degrees of freedom removed by the RG blocking. The lifted configuration provides an informed initial condition for HMC, which removes the residual mismatch with the fine action and remains the final sampler.

This construction separates the sampling problem across scales. Long-distance fluctuations and topological sectors can be sampled on a coarser lattice where transitions remain accessible, while the diffusion model reconstructs the degrees of freedom required on the finer lattice. The procedure can then be applied recursively, propagating coarse-lattice information toward smaller
lattice spacings where direct HMC becomes increasingly inefficient. We show that
a preconditioned chain begins an order of magnitude closer to the target than a
cold one and arrives already carrying a topological charge that plain HMC cannot
reach within any practical budget, and which a classical chain reaches only by
adding a dedicated global update; and that where such a sampler exists the
saving in trajectories is modest rather than large. As controlled test beds, we study two-dimensional U(1) and U(2) lattice gauge theories. These theories contain compact gauge fields and nontrivial
topological sectors, while U(2) additionally provides a non-Abelian setting.
At the same time, the relevant observables can be compared with exact
analytical results, allowing the complete coarse-to-fine procedure to be
validated directly against the target theory.

Machine-learning approaches to lattice sampling have developed rapidly in recent years. Normalizing flows provide explicit and invertible maps between simple prior distributions and lattice field configurations~\cite{albergo2019,abbott2024practical,abbott2025progress}, while diffusion and score-based models provide an alternative class of generative samplers based on stochastic denoising dynamics~\cite{wang2024,zhu2024,zhu2025,aarts2026}. Related developments have also explored stochastic generative constructions~\cite{wang2026stochastic}. These methods have been extended to non-Abelian and group-valued settings,
through gauge-equivariant architectures and score models defined on the group
manifold~\cite{komijani2026,kanwarvega2025,alharazin2026,vega2025}, to theories
with fermions~\cite{vegaelkhadra2026,tomiya2026fermions}, and to models trained
or extrapolated across couplings and lattice
sizes~\cite{tomiya2026}; see~\cite{wenger2026} for a review of the
four-dimensional SU(3) case.

In most of this work the learned model is itself part of the sampler, its output
constituting the generated ensemble either directly or after an
exactness-restoring correction~\cite{aarts2026,sexty2026}, and generalization
beyond the trained parameters has been investigated in the
coupling~\cite{zhu2025,aarts2026} and in the lattice
volume~\cite{tan2026}. The quality of sampling at the target then depends
directly on the accuracy of the model there. Ours is different: the model
constructs only the initial condition, and HMC generates the ensemble.

A second line, closer to the present construction, uses RG information to
initialize fine-lattice chains. RG-matched coarse-to-fine maps followed by
rethermalization reduce the cost relative to cold and hot starts in gauge
theories with and without fermions~\cite{detmoldendres2015,detmoldendres2016},
with the scaling of that advantage toward the continuum examined
in~\cite{detmoldendres2018}; inverse RG transformations have been used to drive
configurations from small lattices to much larger ones~\cite{bachtis2022}, and
the connection between diffusion models and inverse RG flow developed in its own
right~\cite{cotler2023}. More recently the inverse RG step has itself been
learned with conditional normalizing
flows~\cite{hasenfratz2026,hasenfratz2026cascade}, which reach large volumes in
two-dimensional $\phi^4$ theory by iterating it, and related hierarchical
constructions include multilevel and super-resolving
flows~\cite{singha2026multilevel,singha2026pss,bauer2025super}.

We adopt that same separation between coarse sampling, learned upscaling and
fine-lattice rethermalization, but focus on the topological sector. In a gauge
theory the coarse configuration carries not only the long-distance fluctuations
but the sector, and preserving it across the lift initializes fine-lattice HMC
with the right population of sectors without the fine chain having to establish
one through rare tunneling events. A third strategy leaves the ensemble to HMC
but modifies the evolution itself, inserting learned changes of variables inside
trajectories, an idea introduced as an analytic trivializing
map~\cite{luscher2009} and since realized with learned
transformations~\cite{albandea2023,he2025}; generative models have also been
used to weight topological sectors against one another~\cite{singha2026}. We
leave the HMC evolution unchanged.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In \sect{setup}, we define the lattice theories, notation, and conventional initialization
baselines. \sect{method} introduces the RG-conditioned diffusion preconditioner and the recursive coarse-to-fine procedure.
\sect{seedquality} and \sect{coverage} quantify the quality of the generated initial conditions and examine the training coverage required for
reliable transfer across lattice parameters. \sect{conclusions} then discusses what the construction
would require in four-dimensional lattice QCD.


\section{Simulating gauge fields on a two-dimensional lattice}
\label{sec:setup}

The method is demonstrated in two lattice gauge theories, each chosen because it
freezes topologically in the way four-dimensional QCD does while remaining
exactly solvable.
The first is compact U(1), the gauge sector of quantum electrodynamics in two
dimensions. Its compact gauge group gives the field an integer topological
charge and separates the configuration space into sectors. It is also the standard first
testbed for generative models of gauge fields \cite{kanwar2020,zhu2025}. A gauge link carries a group
element on each edge of the lattice; for U(1) that element is a phase,
$U_{x,\mu}=e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}$ with $\theta_{x,\mu}\in(-\pi,\pi]$, one angle
per edge. We follow the conventions of \cite{gattringerlang2010} throughout.

The second theory is two-dimensional U(2). Its structure,
$\mathrm{U}(2)=(\mathrm{U}(1)\times \mathrm{SU}(2))/\mathbb{Z}_2$, has the
product form of the electroweak gauge group
$\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\times \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, though the resemblance is structural
rather than physical. The benefit of U(2) lies in that decomposition. As
\sect{freezing} shows, the topological charge is carried entirely by the
determinant, which is an Abelian U(1) factor, while the gauge dynamics remain
genuinely non-Abelian, each plaquette being an ordered product of matrices.
U(2) therefore separates non-Abelian dynamics from non-Abelian topology, and
serves as a computationally simpler test bed on the way to four-dimensional
SU(3).

A U(2) gauge link is a unitary $2\times2$ matrix, which for computational convenience we
store in the split representation
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,\mu} = e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}
q_1 + i q_4 & q_3 + i q_2 \\
-q_3 + i q_2 & q_1 - i q_4
\end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:u2-link}
\end{equation}
an SU(2) element written as a unit quaternion ($\sum_a q_a^2 = 1$) times a
\emph{central} phase, one proportional to the identity and so commuting with
every SU(2) element. Five real scalars therefore describe a U(2) link.

Every observable is built from the plaquette
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,p} \equiv U_{x,\mu} U_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} U_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu}^\dagger U_{x,\nu}^\dagger,
    \label{eq:plaquette}
\end{equation}
the smallest closed loop, whose trace is therefore gauge invariant; products of adjacent
plaquettes form the larger Wilson loops used as observables. In U(1) the
plaquette reduces to a signed sum of link angles,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{x,p} \equiv \theta_{x,\mu} + \theta_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} - \theta_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu} - \theta_{x,\nu},
    \label{eq:theta-plaquette}
\end{equation}
whose order is immaterial; in U(2) the matrix ordering matters. The Wilson
actions,
\begin{align}
    S_{U(1)} &= -\beta \sum_{x}\cos(\theta_{x,p}), \label{eq:action-u1}\\
    S_{U(2)} &= -\beta \sum_{x} \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}(U_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:action-u2}
\end{align}
are gauge invariant since they depend only on plaquettes.

\subsection{Topological freezing}
\label{sec:freezing}

The topological charge is the wrapped plaquette sum,
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{x} \mathrm{wrap}\,(\theta_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:q-u1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{wrap}(\alpha)$ returns the representative of $\alpha$ in
$(-\pi,\pi]$.\footnote{Evaluated as the two-argument arctangent of
$(\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)$, which fixes the quadrant and so the branch.}

The topological susceptibility is the charge variance per unit volume,
\begin{equation}
    \chi_t = \frac{\langle Q^2\rangle}{V},
    \label{eq:chi-t}
\end{equation}
so $\chi_t V = \langle Q^2\rangle$ measures how many sectors an ensemble
populates \cite{bonatirossi2019}.

For U(2), $Q$ is defined by the same formula applied to the determinant plaquette
phase, $\alpha_x = \mathrm{wrap}(\arg\det U_{x,p})$. The central phases in
\Eq{plaquette} are scalars, so they factor out of the product and combine into
exactly the U(1) plaquette sum of \Eq{theta-plaquette}; the SU(2) factors have
unit determinant and drop out of it entirely. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \det(U_{x,p})=e^{2i\theta_{x,p}}.
    \label{eq:det-plaquette}
\end{equation}
The SU(2) component therefore contributes nothing to $Q$. We call the U(1) component of a U(2) link
its determinant sector, $\psi \equiv \arg\det U = \mathrm{wrap}(2\theta)$.

Since $Q$ is defined by the wrapped sum of plaquettes, it can only change if some
$\theta_{x,p}$ is pushed past $\pm\pi$. At large $\beta$ every plaquette angle
clusters near zero due to the Wilson action in \Eq{action-u1}.
Changing $Q$ through purely local updates therefore requires passing through an
intermediate configuration with some $\theta_{x,p}$ near $\pm\pi$, which costs
$\Delta S = 2\beta$ and is accepted with probability $\sim e^{-2\beta}$. That exponential
suppression is the root cause of topological freezing, and it worsens toward the
continuum limit as $\beta$ grows. By \Eq{det-plaquette} the mechanism
is identical in U(2).

\subsection{Classical baseline}
\label{sec:instanton_updates_classical}
Configurations are generated by HMC \cite{duane1987,neal2011}, with the Omelyan
integrator \cite{omelyan2003} in both, the position update being an exponential
map for the
group-valued U(2) fields. The accept--reject step on the whole trajectory leaves
the chain ergodic at every coupling, so what fails toward the continuum is the
time a sector change takes and not its reachability; \emph{frozen} means
throughout that a chain does not traverse sectors within the budget it is given.

That time can be decreased in 2D U(1) by a global update that jumps between
sectors and is accepted by the same criterion \cite{albandea2021}. We use that
construction with a different proposal: an instanton field $I$ carrying one unit
of charge spread uniformly over the torus, $2\pi/L^2$ in every plaquette, at a cost
$\beta V(1-\cos2\pi/V)\approx2\pi^2\beta/V$ that falls with volume. We call the
result winding HMC. It is the classical baseline of record here and what the
improvement factor of \sect{seedquality} is measured against; plain periodic HMC
is carried alongside as the generic sampler. It delays the freezing barrier to
much higher $\beta$ without eliminating it (\tb{classical}).

In U(2) the same shift is available but reaches only half the sectors. A central
phase shift commutes with SU(2) and costs the same $2\pi^2\beta/V$, but the
determinant sector is $\psi=2\theta$, so the shift moves $Q$ by exactly two and
can never change its parity. Halving it moves $Q$ by one, but leaves a single
plaquette carrying a spurious $\mathbb{Z}_2$ element, and we measure that cost
to be set by $\beta$ alone, so unlike the central shift it does not become cheap
on a large lattice. Smooth instanton solutions in two-dimensional
$\mathrm{U}(N)$, and the parity-dependent constraint their ansatz carries, are
given in \cite{rouenhoff2025}. We call the first the \emph{even-only} move and a crossing between the two
parity classes a \emph{parity flip}. Parity flips recorded against the even-only
move in \tb{classical} are therefore ones the underlying HMC achieves unaided,
by pushing a plaquette angle past $\pm\pi$, and they disappear with the local
freezing of \sect{freezing} rather than with anything the update does. Parity is reached instead
by a different move, which shifts the link phase by half the central instanton,
$\pm I/2$, so that the determinant sector $\psi=2\theta$ moves by $\pm I$ and
$Q$ by one; what distinguishes it is that the SU(2) sector is then regenerated
rather than carried along. It is accepted on the marginal weight $w_{\det}$ of \Eq{wdet}
alone, since the SU(2) sector is about to be replaced, after which $q$ is
resampled from $p(q\mid\psi')$ exactly by heatbath
\cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985}. We call this the \emph{marginal} move,
and it is the winding move used wherever U(2) results are reported.

At $L=64$ and $\beta=416.524$, $200$ cold-started trajectories
over $64$ chains give zero accepted parity flips under the even-only move, so the
measured odd-sector weight is $P(\mathrm{odd})=0$ against an exact $0.4929$.
Under the marginal move the same budget gives $7661$ flips and
$P(\mathrm{odd})=0.4939$. \tb{classical} makes the same comparison at $L=16$ across three
couplings, there from a hot start over $256$ chains.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
Where the baselines of \sect{setup} repair topology inside a running chain, the
method here acts once, before the chain starts. It is one inverse
renormalization-group step, applied repeatedly: a coarse configuration at
$L_c\times L_c$ and coupling $\beta_c$ is lifted to a fine one at $L_f=2L_c$ and
$\beta_f\approx4\beta_c$, with the fine topological charge transported exactly
from the coarse configuration and only the remaining ultraviolet structure
supplied by the model. We call one such $(L,\beta)$ point a \emph{rung}, and the
sequence of rungs running from the base ensemble up to the target the
\emph{ladder}. Before describing the step we fix the direction in which it is
applied, because the topological content of the theory depends on that choice.

The ladder climbs the $(L,\beta)$ plane in one particular direction, and the
choice matters because $P(Q)$ behaves oppositely along the two. Doubling $L$
while quadrupling $\beta$ holds the physical extent $La$ fixed, since
$\beta=1/(e^2a^2)$ sets the spacing \cite{gattringerlang2010}. That is a
continuum limit at fixed physical volume, and along it $\langle Q^2\rangle$
tends to $e^2V_{\rm phys}/4\pi^2$, a finite physical quantity. Transporting the charge is therefore the correct operation along the
ladder, and $P(Q)$ is the same distribution at every rung up to lattice-spacing
corrections. \sect{coverage} evaluates the fixed-$L$ direction instead, one
independent lift per coupling, and never carries a charge across it.

\subsection{Training data}
\label{sec:naive-blocking}


Blocking maps a fine configuration to a coarse one at negligible cost, but it
is not invertible: many fine
configurations block to the same coarse one. The information that many-to-one
map destroys is what the model supplies, and it is
also why a blocking map alone would not do. HMC repairs short-distance
structure quickly, so a crude inverse would be enough if local observables were
all that mattered; what HMC cannot repair within any practical budget is the
topological sector, and the sector is exactly what the coarse configuration
already carries.

A fine configuration is generated by HMC at the rung's own coupling, with the
sector coverage of the high-$\beta$ rungs supplied as described below rather
than sampled. Its coarse partner is fixed completely by summing the neighboring links,
\begin{align*}
\theta_x'(X,Y) &= \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_x(2X,2Y) + \theta_x(2X+1,2Y)\big),\\
\theta_y'(X,Y) &= \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_y(2X,2Y) + \theta_y(2X,2Y+1)\big),
\end{align*}
with $\theta'$ the coarse link angle. In U(2) the same map acts on $\psi$, and
the statement survives the non-Abelian group for one reason: the determinant is
a homomorphism, so the determinant phase of a product of links is the plain sum
of their phases, and \Eq{det-plaquette} telescopes exactly as the Abelian case
does. This makes the coarse plaquette angle equal
the wrapped sum of its four constituent fine sub-plaquettes exactly, so
$Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} = Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ whenever each such sum already lies in
$(-\pi,\pi]$ and the outer wrap is the identity; a cell whose four fine
plaquettes sum past $\pi$ costs the coarse charge one unit. Downward, that
condition is a property of the configuration. Upward it is not relied on: the
lift \emph{imposes} the coarse charge on its output
(\sect{reverse_diffusion}), and we measure the imposition to succeed on every
configuration.

Sector transport is an identity, so whatever $P(Q)$ the base ensemble carries is what
every rung above it receives, and the number of sectors it populates is
$\chi_t V$ of \Eq{chi-t}. We therefore fix $\beta_f$ at each step as the root
of
\begin{equation}
    \langle Q^2\rangle_{\beta_f,\,L_f} \;=\; \langle Q^2\rangle_{\beta_c,\,L_c},
    \qquad L_f = 2L_c,
    \label{eq:topology-matching}
\end{equation}
with both sides evaluated in the closed form of \app{exact}. A solution exists
because to leading order $\chi_t V\approx V/(4\pi^2\beta)$, so doubling $L$
quadruples $V$ while quadrupling $\beta$ restores it, and the root sits near
that tree-level estimate; \app{matching} gives the numerical solution and the
resulting schedules.

At high $\beta$ the fine ensemble used to build training pairs is itself frozen
in $Q$, with $\langle Q^2\rangle\ll1$, so HMC returns configurations almost
entirely at $Q=0$. A training set built purely from HMC output at a fixed high
coupling would then never show the network the local structure of a
configuration at nonzero $Q$ at that coupling.
We manufacture the missing coverage rather than sampling it. Sector
augmentation takes a fixed fraction of the fine ensemble at a rung, draws a
charge shift uniformly from a small integer range, adds that many copies of the
instanton field of \sect{instanton_updates_classical}, and relaxes the result
locally at fixed topology to remove the ultraviolet error the instanton leaves
behind. That range is fixed, with no reference to $\beta$ or to the sector
weights at the coupling, so the coverage itself is manufactured without a closed
form. The six U(2) rungs that carry augmentation are in addition started from
sectors drawn from the exact $P(Q)$ of \app{exact}, which sets how often each
sector appears but not whether charged configurations appear at all. That
seeding is a convenience rather than a requirement: replacing it by a uniform
draw over the same sectors leaves the lifted ensemble's agreement unchanged to
within the resolution of that comparison, which excludes a large dependence on
the sector frequencies and not a small one (\app{sectors}). What the training
data has to supply is sector coverage rather than correct sector frequencies.

\subsection{Score network}
\label{sec:scoring-network}

\begin{figure*}[bt]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{u1_2d/45_architecture.png}
    \caption{The gauge-covariant score network, shown for U(1), with
  $324{,}954$ parameters in $4$ FiLM blocks of width $56$. The U(2) network has
  the same structure, with width $64$ and $403{,}394$ parameters.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

Diffusion models generate by reversing a noising process. A forward It\^o SDE
$dx = f(x,t)dt + g(t)dW$ carries the data distribution to a tractable reference
over $t\in[0,T]$. By Anderson's theorem \cite{anderson1982} it has a
reverse-time companion with the same marginals, whose only unknown is the score
$\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$; denoising score matching \cite{vincent2011} estimates
that score by regressing on the known conditional transition kernel, and Song et
al.\ \cite{song2021} supply the discretizations used here. The standard
construction assumes $x\in\mathbb R^n$ with a Gaussian kernel. Neither holds for
link angles, which live on a compact periodic domain, so both the forward
process and the score it induces are built from the domain's own kernel.

Each configuration is a vector $\vec\theta$ with $2L^2$ components, noised
independently with the wrapped Gaussian transition kernel
\begin{equation}
    K(\theta;\hat\theta,\sigma_t)=\frac{1}{\sigma_t\sqrt{2\pi}}
      \sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty}
      \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\theta-\hat{\theta} + 2\pi r)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right),
    \label{eq:wrapped-kernel}
\end{equation}
with $\hat\theta$ the clean angle. As $\sigma_t$ grows this approaches the uniform
distribution on the circle, and the regression target is $\nabla_\theta\log K$.

The architecture is shown in \fig{architecture}. The network sees two fields,
the noisy fine configuration and the coarse configuration conditioning it, and
sees both only through small Wilson loops rather than through raw link angles;
\app{settings} lists the channels. The coarse field is enlarged to the fine grid
by nearest-neighbor replication before the two are concatenated. The noise level $\sigma_t$ and
coupling $\beta$ enter through a sinusoidal embedding feeding per-block
Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018}, into which the
spatial mean of the coarse conditioning channels is also projected. That gives
every block direct access to a genuinely global quantity, since the mean wrapped
coarse plaquette angle equals $2\pi Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}/V$ by definition
of $Q$, and a convolutional network with bounded kernel size cannot otherwise
reconstruct a lattice-wide sum.

Gauge invariance is built into the architecture, by the construction of
\Eq{curl-x} and \Eq{curl-y} given below. The Wilson action's
$90^\circ$-rotation, reflection and charge-conjugation symmetries are not, and
are supplied by augmenting the training data instead (\app{settings}).

The network predicts one scalar $h_p$ per plaquette rather than a link-valued
score, and the score is assembled by the lattice curl,
\begin{align}
  s_x(x,y) &= h_p(x,y) - h_p(x,y-1), \label{eq:curl-x}\\
  s_y(x,y) &= h_p(x-1,y) - h_p(x,y). \label{eq:curl-y}
\end{align}
This is the general form of the gradient of any sum of functions of plaquettes:
each link belongs to exactly two plaquettes with opposite orientation, so
$\partial\theta_p/\partial\theta_\ell\in\{+1,-1,0\}$, every such gradient is a
lattice curl of some per-plaquette scalar, and every per-plaquette scalar
produces one. Because $h_p$ is itself a function of gauge-invariant inputs, the
score is gauge-covariant by construction. The curl can therefore represent the
score of the Wilson density exactly. What it cannot represent is a component
along the torus holonomies, which the conditional does carry, since blocking
fixes the coarse Polyakov loops from the fine ones; no observable reported here
is sensitive to that direction, all of them being functionals of plaquettes. Nor does any part of the network refer to the
lattice size: the convolutions are circular and the normalization acts on each
site separately, so one trained network applies at every volume on the ladder,
which is what \sect{u1obs} tests directly.

The analytic Wilson force drawn alongside the learned head in
\fig{architecture} is internal to the network and present in both theories: the
exact small-$\sigma$ score is $\sigma$ times the curl of $-\beta\sin\theta_p$,
and the head adds that term under a gate learned as a function of the
$(\sigma,\beta)$ embedding, initialized at zero. It is distinct from the
sampler-level blend of \sect{reverse_diffusion}, which acts on the score during
sampling and is switched off in U(2) for the reason \app{blend} measures.

Network capacity, the training budget and the couplings each checkpoint was
trained on, together with the sampler settings used
throughout, are collected in \app{settings}.

\subsection{Reverse diffusion process}
\label{sec:reverse_diffusion}

\begin{figure*}[bt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reverse_diffusion_schematic.png}
    \caption{The reverse-diffusion chain for 2D U(1) and U(2)
    (\sect{reverse_diffusion}). It runs from right to left, starting from angles
    $\phi_T$ drawn uniformly on the circle and ending at the lifted configuration
    $\phi_0$, with $\phi$ standing for whichever angle the model denoises: the
    link angle $\theta$ in U(1) and the determinant phase $\psi$ in U(2). Here
    $s_\theta$ is the learned score and
    $\Delta_t=\sigma_t^2-\sigma_{t-1}^2$, and each denoising step is followed by
    one Langevin correction step (dashed).
In U(2) the SU(2) sector is then drawn
    conditionally by the exact heatbath of \sect{u2-extension}, which generates
    it at fixed $\psi$. Nothing further is applied: the result is what HMC
    receives.}
    \label{fig:method_schematic}
\end{figure*}

The wrapped-Gaussian kernel is exactly the transition kernel of a driftless
diffusion wrapped onto the circle, so $f\equiv0$ and
$g(t)=\sqrt{d\sigma_t^2/dt}$: a variance-exploding schedule in the terminology of
\cite{song2021}, whose behavior on a group manifold is examined in
\cite{komijani2026noise}. The forward process is $d\theta = g(t)dW$ and its
reverse-time companion is
\begin{equation}
    d\theta = -g(t)^2\,s_\theta(\theta_t,\sigma_t,\beta)\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar W,
    \label{eq:reverse-sde}
\end{equation}
so the drift of the reverse process is the learned score. In U(1) one further
term enters: the analytic Wilson score of the target action, evaluated at a
noise-smeared coupling and blended in with a weight that vanishes at large
$\sigma$ and rises as $\sigma$ falls, so that the network supplies the drift
while the field is noisy and the known action takes over as it becomes clean.
A second, $\sigma$-independent factor $[1+(\beta_{\min}/\beta)^2]^{-1}$ caps
that weight at low coupling, where the near-Gaussian limit behind the smeared
coupling is poor: with $\beta_{\min}=5$ the small-$\sigma$ weight is $0.39$ at
the first rung and $0.99$ at the last, so the analytic term takes over only at
the stiff end of the ladder. The U(2) sampler uses the learned score alone: an
analogous blend is implemented there, is deployed at zero weight, and degrades every
observable when it is switched on, for reasons that are not settled
(\app{blend}). Starting from pure noise
on the fine lattice, ancestral sampling integrates this backward through the
noise schedule (\fig{method_schematic}). Every result reported here takes one
Langevin corrector step after each ancestral step \cite{song2021}.

Three mechanisms softly bias $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ toward $Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$
during sampling: the coarse-conditioning channels, the FiLM-embedded global
mean, and a reconstruction-guidance penalty \cite{ho2022} added to the score at each noise
level. That penalty is the gradient, with respect to the cell sum, of
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\big(\sum_{p=1}^4\theta_{p,\mathrm{fine}}
        - \theta_{\mathrm{coarse}}\big)^2}{2v(\sigma)},
    \qquad v(\sigma)\approx 8\sigma^2,
    \label{eq:guidance}
\end{equation}
in which $v(\sigma)$ is the noise that the eight boundary links of a coarse
cell inject into the blocked plaquette at level $\sigma$. The per-cell scalar is broadcast to the four fine sub-plaquettes and
assembled into link space by the same discrete curl used for the score head, so
the guidance is gauge-covariant by construction. None of these make $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}=Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$ exact, they only
make the sampler more likely to settle in the correct sector on its own.

The equality $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}=Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$ is instead imposed
exactly by a fourth step: the sampler computes
$\Delta Q = Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} - Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ and adds $\Delta Q$ copies
of the instanton field of \sect{instanton_updates_classical}. It does this at every tenth reverse-SDE step once the noise level
has fallen below $\sigma=0.5$, and once more unconditionally after sampling
completes.\footnote{A stated setting rather than a fitted one: above it the charge of the
noisy sample is not yet meaningful to project onto. Projecting partway through
the reverse process rather than only at the end leaves the remaining steps to
relax the ultraviolet error the projection introduces.}
Nothing repairs the configuration after sampling. In U(1) the output of the
reverse chain is what HMC receives; in U(2) the conditional SU(2) heatbath of
\sect{u2-extension} runs first, but that generates a sector the model does not
produce rather than correcting one it does, and it leaves $\psi$, and hence $Q$,
unchanged. Local repair is left to HMC, so that what the model supplies and what
a local algorithm supplies are never mixed in one number.

The charge a preconditioned chain begins with is therefore inherited rather than
sampled, and the argument does not require that inheritance to be exact. HMC is
ergodic (\sect{instanton_updates_classical}), so any distortion of the sector
weights would eventually decay; transport does not replace that guarantee, it
avoids having to spend the trajectories that realizing it would cost. The chain
starts in the sector distribution it would otherwise have had to reach, and the
accept--reject step remains responsible for correctness throughout.

\subsection{Extension to U(2)}
\label{sec:u2-extension}

Since the topology of 2D U(2) is carried by the determinant and the theory is
exactly solvable \cite{bonatirossi2019}, the construction carries over with
$\psi$ in place of $\theta$: the diffusion model generates only the determinant
sector. Integrating SU(2) out of one plaquette by the one-link integral
\cite{creutz1980} gives the marginal weight of the determinant plaquette angle,
\begin{equation}
    w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta) = \frac{2I_1(z)}{z},
    \qquad z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2),
    \label{eq:wdet}
\end{equation}
which is not of Wilson form, so the network is conditioned on the matched U(1)
coupling $\beta_1(\beta)$ defined in \app{exact} (asymptotically $\beta/4$)
rather than on $\beta$. Every coupling quoted in this paper is the raw U(2)
$\beta$ unless named otherwise.\footnote{The one exception is the checkpoint
name \texttt{cov60}, which refers to $\beta_1\approx57$, or $\beta=227$ raw.}
The SU(2) sector needs no model: the action does not factorize, but at fixed
$\psi$ each SU(2) link has the standard single-link weight, so heatbath sweeps
\cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985} draw it exactly from
$p(q\mid\psi)$ while leaving $\psi$, and hence $Q$, unchanged.


\section{Preconditioner quality}
\label{sec:seedquality}
What the preconditioner is worth is measured in two ways: how close the
configurations it delivers are to the exact target distribution, and what a chain
started from one costs. We call the output of one inverse
renormalization-group step a \emph{lifted configuration}: the reverse-diffusion
output, together with the conditional SU(2) heatbath in U(2). It is both what
the map produces and what HMC is handed, so every number below refers to the
same object.
An \emph{arm} is one complete run: a cold, hot or preconditioned start advanced
by one named sampler for a stated budget. 

The scored observables are Wilson loops from the plaquette to $8\times8$,
probing both ultraviolet (UV) and infrared structure, together with $P(Q)$ and
$\chi_t$; all follow from the single-plaquette character coefficients collected
in \app{exact}. A Wilson loop is quoted as its relative deviation from exact in
parts per million (ppm), a charge variance as a ratio to its exact value,
because the arms run from a frozen chain at zero to a hot start at $88$ times
exact and a relative deviation is unreadable across that range.

\subsection{Two timescales}
\label{sec:relaxation-correction}
Two timescales are needed, in the spirit of the multiscale equilibration studies
of \cite{detmoldendres2015,detmoldendres2016}. The cost of one independent
configuration from a running chain is $t_{\rm interval}=2\tau_{\rm int}$, with
the integrated autocorrelation time estimated by Sokal's automatic windowing
\cite{madrassokal1988,wolff2004} as specified in \app{stats}. We maximize it
over the plaquette, $W(2\times2)$ and $W(4\times4)$ \emph{and} over $Q^2$, so an
arm whose charge never moves has no finite interval.

The approach to equilibrium is set instead by the slowest mode of the transition
matrix and by the starting distribution's overlap with it
\cite{detmoldendres2015}. Fitting that decay, the natural estimator when the
start is far from equilibrium, is not available here, because a preconditioned
chain can begin at the target and leave no transient to fit. We therefore read the equilibration time off the data: $t_{\rm therm}$ is the
first trajectory after which the bias against exact stays within two standard
errors for five consecutive measurements, maximized over those same three loops
but not over $Q^2$, which would make every frozen classical arm infinite and the
comparison empty. On the preconditioned side the exclusion costs nothing, since
its charge is imposed by transport rather than approached by the chain. Those two standard errors are the error on the ensemble mean
at one record, taken across chains (\app{stats}), so the band narrows as the
per-configuration spread $\sigma_{\rm cfg}$ does: at fixed coupling
$\sigma_{\rm cfg}\propto V^{-1/2}$ for a self-averaging observable, and along
the ladder, where $\beta$ rises with $L$, it falls faster still. A
$t_{\rm therm}$ is therefore a crossing against a yardstick that depends on the
volume and coupling at which it was measured, and values at different rungs are
not comparable at face value. \app{stats} gives the scaling and what it implies
for the ladder.
Topology is scored separately against the closed form. Both timescales carry the
chain-resampling interval of \app{stats}.

The two timescales enter the cost of an ensemble differently. A classical chain
equilibrates once and then yields one configuration per interval, so its
per-configuration cost tends to the interval as the ensemble grows. A
preconditioned ensemble is built the other way round: each configuration
descends from an independent coarse one, so successive members owe no interval
to one another, and the cost is the equilibration each of them pays. Comparing
the two gives the improvement factor,
\begin{equation}
    F = \frac{t_{\rm interval}\ (\text{classical})}
             {t_{\rm therm}\ (\text{preconditioned})},
    \label{eq:improvement-factor}
\end{equation}
and it is what we report throughout.

\subsection{U(1)}
\label{sec:u1obs}
The lift does not arrive at the target. Across the five couplings tested
($\beta_f=4.44$ to $218.58$, a $50\times$ range) its plaquette sits between
$3.0$ and $14.2$ standard errors from exact before any trajectory, so the local
structure is HMC's to repair and is charged for in trajectories. What the lift
does arrive with is the charge: $\langle Q^2\rangle$ comes out $6.62(78)$,
$1.80(19)$, $0.406(51)$, $0.180(34)$ and $0.039(17)$ against exact values of
$6.795$, $1.904$, $0.474$, $0.171$ and $0.029$, every one within $1.4$ standard
errors, transported from a coarse ensemble sampled where sampling
works. Winding HMC, by contrast, reaches the quality criterion of \tb{u1_h2h}
within a $500$-trajectory burn-in at the two smallest of those couplings
($\beta_f=4.44$, hot-started, and $14.1464$) but not at $\beta_f=55.02$, where
it fails at $500$ and at $2000$ and passes at $8000$, nor at $\beta_f=218.58$,
where it fails at all three. That burn-in scan is a separate experiment over
four of the five couplings, so \tb{u1_h2h} has no row at $\beta_f=118.5$. All of
them are an independent
$L=16\to32$ grid rather than rungs of the ladder, each scored on its own with no
charge carried between them.

The map itself survives a change of volume, as the architecture predicts: at
fixed $\beta_f=14.1464$ over $L=32$, $64$ and $128$ the lift's plaquette
sits $869$, $892$ and $820$ parts per million from exact, flat in volume.
Nothing between the lift and HMC alters the charge, so what the lift delivers is
what HMC receives. Against exact,
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ is $0.95\pm0.10$, $1.20\pm0.18$ and $0.71\pm0.13$ at the
three volumes; the first two are consistent and the third low by $2.3$ standard
errors, the only one in this scan not reproduced within its error, and at
$L=128$ the lift draws on a coarse ensemble two doublings above the base, so we
do not resolve whether the deficit is the coarse ensemble's or the map's.
Winding HMC is the comparison that matters here and it samples topology
correctly, reaching $0.98$ times exact (\tb{classical}): on this observable the
two are indistinguishable at this coupling, and what separates them is cost
(\sect{cost}).

The equilibration advantage is real but volume-dependent. Over the same three
volumes the preconditioned chain needs $9$, $18$ and $52$ trajectories against a
cold start's $168$, $400$ and $528$ under the same winding HMC, ratios of
$t_{\rm therm}$ of $19$, $22$ and $10$. These compare two equilibration times
and are not the improvement factor of \Eq{improvement-factor}, whose numerator
is a decorrelation interval. Both arms lengthen with volume because $t_{\rm therm}$ is a crossing in units of a
standard error, which shrinks as the volume grows, so a deviation of fixed
relative size requires more trajectories to settle inside the band.

\subsection{U(2)}
\label{sec:u2obs}
The U(2) benchmark is where the two failure modes separate most cleanly, because
at $L=64$, $\beta=416.5$ the classical arms fail at UV structure and at topology
independently, and no classical arm repairs both. What this benchmark does and
does not extrapolate should be read off the training set first. Both couplings
it uses are in it: the coarse ensemble sits at $\beta=105.651$ and the fine
target at $\beta=416.524$, and the deployed checkpoint was trained at each, on
$L=32$ lattices. Since the network is conditioned on the coupling and not on the
volume, what is being tested here is transfer in volume at a trained coupling,
and nothing about coupling extrapolation. The $L=128$ result of
\sect{multi-lift} adds the second kind, its $\beta=1660.08$ falling between the
training rungs of the checkpoint used there, at $1565$ and $1686$, rather than
on one, and
\sect{coverage} varies coverage deliberately. \tb{u2_observables} gives each
arm's mean Wilson loop after $400$ trajectories on $64$ chains, averaged over
the settled half of every history and compared with the closed form; the exact
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ is $1.0012$. Every arm started from a lifted
configuration is close on both counts: at $L=64$ the
plaquette lies within $6.2$~ppm of exact and $W(2\times2)$ within $21$~ppm,
against a cold start's $51$ and $229$~ppm under the same sampler, and
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ falls between $0.97$ and $1.14$ times exact where every
cold arm under plain HMC is at zero. The upper end is
the plain-HMC arm, whose charge is inherited from the preconditioner and cannot
move, so that $1.14$ is a single frozen value rather than a sampled distribution. No
classical arm reaches both the UV and the topological target.

The uncertainties change how the two volumes should be read. At $L=64$ the
lifted arms sit between $2.2$ and $3.2$ standard errors from exact on the
plaquette, a deviation of $4$ to $6$~ppm, and within two standard errors on
$W(2\times2)$; at $L=32$ the lattice is a quarter the size and the large loops
carry a per-configuration spread wide enough that none of the lifted arms'
$W(8\times8)$ deviations are resolved at all. The $-9628$~ppm of the plain-HMC
arm is the largest of them and is $1.8$ standard errors, so it is a fluctuation
rather than a defect of the map. The cold arms are no better resolved on that
loop, two of the three also sitting under two standard errors, so what separates
the two kinds of start at this volume is the plaquette: every cold arm is six
standard errors from exact there, and every lifted arm within $2.4$.

Still at $L=64$, a cold start under plain HMC and the same budget is $51$, $229$ and
$7644$~ppm high on those three loops. Comparing each cold arm with the
lifted arm that ran the same sampler, the cold start is $8$ to $37$ times
further from exact on the plaquette and $W(2\times2)$. Its topological
charge never moves in the $400$ trajectories, so its $\langle Q^2\rangle$ is
exactly zero times exact. Adding the winding move
repairs the charge but not the local observables: $\langle Q^2\rangle$ comes
back to $0.98\pm0.03$ of exact under the marginal move, while the plaquette
stays at $56$~ppm. A hot start does not begin to equilibrate within the
budget: it is $62{,}500$~ppm low on the plaquette and $994{,}000$~ppm low on
$W(8\times8)$. It carries $\langle Q^2\rangle$ at $88$ times exact.

\subsection{Cost accounting}
\label{sec:cost}
An interval measured on a chain that has not equilibrated counts decorrelation
within the sector it started in rather than a supply of independent
configurations of the target, which is why a plaquette interval looks shortest
on precisely the arms that are worst off. Equilibration is therefore a gate on
the interval: where a classical arm does not pass it within the budget we take
its cost as infinite and report the improvement factor as a lower bound, that
bound being the coupling's own budget (\tb{grid}) divided by the preconditioned
cost. The gate is a first crossing against a per-record band, so at large volume
it can admit an arm whose settled mean is still many standard errors from exact;
that error gives the classical arm a finite cost where it should have none and
so lowers $F$, which is the direction that does not flatter the method. What $F$
counts on both sides is HMC trajectories. The cost of building the
lifted configuration falls outside that count, and it is not small: in
U(1), where the classical baseline still converges, the campaign that produced
the checkpoint costs more than every classical burn-in that converges, and a
lifted configuration costs more than a trajectory, so measured in seconds the
method loses at every coupling where the classical baseline is correct; in U(2)
a configuration from the full ladder, base generation included, costs $2.4$
times what a winding-HMC configuration costs, and $1.4$ times counting the top
rung alone; the per-rung wall-clock these are formed from was measured once and
not re-timed since, so treat both as indicative rather than precise. $F$ is therefore not a wall-clock speed-up, and the case for the method rests on
the regime where no classical budget is sufficient rather than on cost in the
regime where one is (\app{overhead}).

\fig{money} measures the improvement factor against winding HMC across
the coupling range, at both evaluated volumes, and the result separates into
two regimes.

Every case in which winding HMC equilibrates lies below $\beta\approx90$, and
there the improvement factor is measured rather than bounded: eighteen such
cases, spanning six distinct couplings across the three checkpoints of
\fig{money} and the two volumes. It is modest, with a median of $1.4$, a range
of $0.1$ to $18$, and a value below one in six of the eighteen. Where the lifted
chain is already equilibrated at $t=0$ we count its cost as one trajectory
rather than zero, which keeps the ratio finite at the price of understating it;
this applies to three of these eighteen and to one of the bounds below, and in
each case it is the largest value quoted that is held down. Counted in
trajectories alone and against the strongest classical sampler this theory
admits, the preconditioner is break-even at best, which is the expected result
in a regime where a good classical sampler already exists. The converse does not
hold: winding HMC fails to equilibrate at two couplings below the crossover,
$\beta=41.5$ at $L=64$ and $\beta=58.0$ at $L=32$, which is why \fig{money}
carries lower bounds there as well.

Above $\beta\approx90$ no classical arm reaches equilibrium within the budget
at any coupling tested, so its cost is infinite by the gate above and the
improvement factor is a lower bound, running from $1.5$ to $200$ with a median
of $9.1$ over twenty-four cases. What fails there is not topology: the winding
move keeps firing, and at the stiffest point of the grid, $\beta=1660$ at
$L=64$, it still changes the parity of $Q$ four hundred times over the
budget. It is the local observables that never settle,
so the chain decorrelates without ever equilibrating.

Plain HMC is the weaker baseline and fails earlier. Its interval, maximized over
$Q^2$, is finite only at the two softest couplings of the grid, $\beta=10.9$ and
$13.9$ at $L=32$; from $\beta\approx25$ upward, and at every $L=64$ coupling
including the lowest, its charge does not move within the budget and it supplies
no independent configurations of the target at any cost.


\subsection{Reach in volume}
\label{sec:multi-lift}

Reaching a target $(L,\beta)$ may take several lifts, and the error does not
compound when it does: across four cases, comprising both theories and
endpoints inside and beyond the training range, three lifts to a fixed endpoint
leave $0.84$ to $1.00$ times the deviation from exact that a single lift to the
same endpoint leaves. The error enters at the last rung, so endpoint accuracy is
set by that rung's distance from training coverage and not by the number of
rungs climbed. Laddering therefore extends the volume reached rather than the
coupling, since each lift multiplies $\beta$ by about four and the last one lands
at the same fine coupling whatever path reached it.

The ladder was pushed one rung past the deployed configuration, to $L=128$ at
$\beta=1660.08$, four times the largest linear size in the training set and
sixteen times the sites. Before a single trajectory the lifted configuration sits $35$~ppm from the
exact plaquette against a cold start's $1200$~ppm, and after $400$ trajectories
it reaches $5.7$~ppm against $25$~ppm, both read at the last record as in
\tb{u2_L128}. No arm at this rung meets the
equilibration criterion of \sect{relaxation-correction} within the budget,
the lifted ones included, so no improvement factor is quoted for it: what the
top rung establishes is that the lift starts an order of magnitude closer and
stays closer, not that it equilibrates where the classical arms cannot.

The charge comes through. The arm that isolates transport is the one running
plain HMC, which cannot change the charge it was given: it holds
$\langle Q^2\rangle=0.749\pm0.239$ for the whole run, consistent with exact but
only at the $24\%$ precision $16$ chains of frozen charges allow. Transport is
therefore demonstrated here at that precision and not better. The two arms
carrying the marginal move are still relaxing in $Q$ when the budget ends, one
climbing from the transported $0.75$ to $1.203\pm0.085$ and the other from a
cold start's zero to $1.249\pm0.052$, several hundred parity flips apiece, so
neither has reached the move's stationary distribution and neither bounds it.
That distribution is measured at the rung below, where the same move from a cold
start gives $0.98$ (\tb{u2_observables}).

\section{Training coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

Where the preconditioner degrades is governed by coupling coverage: which
couplings a checkpoint was trained on, and how far the test coupling sits from
them, a question also asked of a conditional flow for two-dimensional U(1)
\cite{singha2023conditional}. A checkpoint's \emph{training
ceiling} is the largest coupling it was ever given training data at; because the
network is conditioned on the fine side of each pair, that is the largest
coupling it was asked to generate \emph{at}, with the coarse side near
$\beta_f/4$.

The experiment is retraining on different sets of couplings, which we call
\emph{training rungs}. Within each theory the checkpoints share architecture,
capacity, optimization and data recipe and differ only in those rungs, so any
change among them is attributable to coverage. Three U(1) checkpoints are
compared, \texttt{deployed} (ceiling $\beta=57$, the one used for every other
U(1) result here), \texttt{wide2000} (ceiling $2000$, $11$ fixed rungs against
$4$) and \texttt{wide2000\_dense} ($30$ further rungs inside that range); and
four U(2) ones, the narrow \texttt{cov60} and \texttt{default} (ceilings $227$
and $416$, the latter deployed everywhere else here), \texttt{wide} (ceiling
$2000$, $23$ rungs against $11$) and \texttt{wide\_dense} ($31$ further rungs
inside it). \texttt{default}'s rungs are not spread over the coupling axis but
placed on the ladder of \sect{naive-blocking}.

Each is scored over the same grid (\app{grid}), run twice, once with plain HMC
in every arm and once with the winding move, so preconditioned and classical
starts are always paired under an identical sampler. \fig{money} draws three of
the four; \tb{volume_scaling} scores all four and
\tb{u1_coverage_tests} gives the matching U(1) retrain, both in \app{tables}.

Every comparison below scores the lift against exact as a relative deviation in
parts per million. We pre-registered the same deviation in units of a standard
error as the primary endpoint and report it alongside throughout, but tabulate
the relative one, and the reason is that these comparisons are paired at fixed
coupling. Two checkpoints scored on the same coupling face the same
per-configuration spread, so their ratio of deviations is a statement about the
two maps alone; dividing each by a standard error first adds a quantity that
varies by orders of magnitude across the grid and rewards a checkpoint for being
noisier at equal bias. The two endpoints leave every ordering in this section
unchanged, and both are given in \tbs{u1_coverage_tests}{u2_coverage_tests}.

Range is the axis that moves the coupling at which the method stops working.
\texttt{default} stops equilibrating
above $\beta=415$ at $L=32$ and above $\beta=94$ at $L=64$, while
\texttt{wide\_dense} works at every coupling tested, to $\beta=1623$ and
$\beta=1660$ respectively. \texttt{wide} does not: at $L=64$ it fails at three couplings inside its own
trained range, $\beta=174$, $637$ and $1660$ under winding HMC and
$\beta=94$, $637$ and $1660$ under the plain one, so range alone does not
guarantee that the coupling reached moves with it. \texttt{wide} is omitted from
\fig{money}, which would be unreadable at four checkpoints, so those six
outcomes are read from the scan records rather than shown.
Density acts below the ceiling instead, and far more weakly: adding $31$ rungs
inside \texttt{wide}'s existing range improves it at $30$ of $44$ cases, by a
median of $2.1\times$ where range gives one to two orders of magnitude, and that
gain is resolved only in the pooled sample, $[1.2,2.7]$, with either half alone
spanning unity. In U(1) density changes nothing measurable, which we attribute
to those sets already being dense: the two checkpoints it separates share an
identical block of $78$ randomly placed couplings below $\beta=250$ and the same
$11$ fixed rungs, so the $30$ between them are a small increment on a filled
grid. The two
axes therefore act where the other does not, and on this evidence a fixed rung
budget is better spent extending the range than resampling what it covers.

The curves in \fig{money} are irregular because $t_{\rm therm}$ is a discrete
first-crossing index, so the level of a curve carries more than any point on it,
and above $\beta\approx90$ every point is a lower bound. That there is no clean
trend in $\beta$ is itself the result. We read it as the factor tracking
distance to the nearest training rung rather than $\beta$ itself, the rungs
sitting at unevenly spaced couplings so that the distance falls and rises again
as $\beta$ passes each in turn; the grid was not designed to test that reading,
which would need couplings placed at controlled distances from known rungs, and
we do not quote a correlation for it.

Two limits bound what this grid can show, and both follow from its holding $L$
fixed while $\beta$ rises, so that successive points target theories of smaller
physical volume. The charge distribution narrows with them: at $L=32$ the exact
$\chi_t V$ falls from $0.22$ at $\beta=415$ to $0.0008$ at $\beta=1623$, where
$999$ configurations in $1000$ carry $Q=0$. At the top of the grid there is
almost no topology left to transport and a cold start is already nearly correct
on that count, so what the improvement factor measures there is local
equilibration alone; the topological claim rests on the ladder, where
\Eq{topology-matching} holds $\chi_t V$ and the physical volume fixed. That also
caps the grid at $\beta\approx1660$, below the wide checkpoints' ceiling of
$2000$, so only the narrow pair is ever tested outside its trained range, where
it fails at all seven couplings above \texttt{default}'s ceiling. The comparison
therefore establishes that a checkpoint fails outside its coverage, but not that
widening helps for any reason beyond bringing the test back inside it;
separating those would need a grid above $\beta=2000$ at $L\approx200$.
\texttt{cov60} differs from the other three in three further training settings
--- it carries neither the symmetry augmentation nor the high-$\beta$ noise bias
of \app{settings}, and leaves the noise floor's coupling scaling at its default
--- so we retrained it with all three restored on the same rungs
and budget rather than assume they do not matter. It is unchanged: the median
ratio of the two raw-lift deviations is $1.00$ and the retrained model is closer
at $7$ of $14$ couplings, so the contrast between \texttt{cov60} and
\texttt{default} is a coverage effect. \texttt{wide\_dense} against
\texttt{wide}, which differ by nothing but the $31$ added rungs, is the
independent check on the same axis. The top rung of \sect{multi-lift} is a
worked instance of it at a coupling chosen for physics rather than to make the
point: $\beta=1660$ falls in a gap of \texttt{wide}'s rung set, whose nearest
neighbours are $1500$ and $1750$, while \texttt{wide\_dense} has one at $1686$.
Lifting to $L=128$ with each in turn, at identical range and identical
everything else, the denser checkpoint starts $35$~ppm from exact against
$68$~ppm --- a factor of two from rungs alone, with the two charge
distributions identical because transport does not consult the model.

That quality is governed by coverage would be a hard limit if coverage were
expensive to buy, and the central thing it is not is \emph{ergodic}. A rung has
to teach the conditional local structure at a given sector, which heatbath and
overrelaxation equilibrate whether or not the chain can tunnel, and it has to
contain configurations at nonzero charge, which the augmentation of
\sect{naive-blocking} manufactures from a fixed instanton shift in both
theories. Neither asks the sampler at that rung's own coupling to change sector,
so a rung can be built exactly where the theory is frozen --- which is where a
correctly distributed ensemble is otherwise unobtainable, and is the reason
topological freezing does not propagate into the training set. What a rung does
still cost is local equilibration, and \app{settings} gives that bill: hundreds
to a couple of thousand trajectories apiece, rising with the coupling. That cost
is paid once per checkpoint rather than once per configuration, but it is
amortized only over the couplings its rungs actually cover, so reaching a new
region of the coupling axis means new rungs and a retrain --- the trade
\tb{u2_coverage_tests} prices.

A ceiling predicts failure only roughly, and errs both ways: \texttt{cov60}
still equilibrates at $\beta=264$, above its own ceiling of $227$, while
\texttt{default} has already failed at $\beta=174$ and $L=64$, under half of
its. The volume step moves that point without harming the map, whose deviation
is smaller at $L=64$ for all four checkpoints (\tb{volume_scaling}); what the
larger volume tightens is the criterion, since an ensemble mean over more sites
carries a smaller error bar.

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusions}
The objective of this program is four-dimensional SU(3) gauge theory and ultimately full QCD with dynamical
fermions,
where topological freezing is a practical limit on the lattice spacings
reachable. The two-dimensional theories in this study are a controlled setting in which
the method can be measured.
The results rest on three structural properties of the two-dimensional theories,
and 4D SU(3) has none of them.

The first is that blocking preserves $Q$ exactly, which holds only because the
plaquette phase is an Abelian sum of link variables, so the four fine
sub-plaquettes telescope into the coarse one; an ordered product of SU(3)
matrices does not telescope. Transport also needs the coarse ensemble's $P(Q)$
to be the fine theory's already, since an exact map applied to a charge from the
wrong distribution reproduces the wrong distribution exactly; in two dimensions
\Eq{topology-matching} supplies that automatically. In 4D the principle survives
at fixed physical volume but neither part stays exact: couplings would have to
be matched numerically by scale setting \cite{sommer1994,luscher2010}, and $Q$
itself has no cheap unambiguous lattice definition, the field-theoretic form
requiring a smoothing whose scheme affects the result \cite{alexandrou2015} and
the index of a Ginsparg--Wilson operator being exact but expensive
\cite{hasenfratz1998}. Imposing $Q_{\rm coarse}$ would then be
exact only with respect to a chosen definition.

The second is an inexpensive update that changes $Q$, which the method uses
twice: to manufacture sector coverage in the training data and to impose
$Q_{\rm coarse}$ on the lifted configuration. It exists in U(2) because the
group decomposes into a U(1) factor and a topologically trivial SU(2) one, with
the charge carried entirely by the Abelian piece. SU(3) has no such
decomposition, and a cheap topology-changing update for it is the central open
question here. Mechanisms that could supply one exist: parallel tempering on boundary conditions, introduced for the two-dimensional
$\mathrm{CP}^{N-1}$ model \cite{hasenbusch2017} and since carried to
four-dimensional $\mathrm{SU}(N)$ and to full QCD
\cite{bonanno2021,bonanno2024}; the related strategy of opening the boundary to
admit topological change and then restoring periodicity by a non-equilibrium
evolution, now driven by a stochastic normalizing flow, which has been scaled to
four-dimensional SU(3) \cite{bonanno2025su3,bonanno2026}; a stochastic dynamics able to jump
between sectors outright \cite{rothkopf2026}; or a deliberate bias of the
generating action, a chemical potential $-\mu Q$ or a bias potential in $Q$ as in
multicanonical sampling \cite{bergneuhaus1992} and metadynamics
\cite{laio2016,eichhorn2026}. We regard the last of these as the most promising, since it is the cheapest and
asks the least of the mechanism. A bias potential ordinarily has to be
reweighted away, because it distorts the distribution the sampler produces and
that distribution is the result. Here it is not: the bias would act only while
training data is built, and only to visit sectors, so on the evidence of
\app{sectors} neither its acceptance rate nor the sector frequencies it produces
would have to be accurate. The charge is
imposed by transport at deployment, and what the model must learn is the local
structure at a given charge.

The third property is exact solvability, and its loss would cost more than the
ability to score the results. The thermalization time $t_{\rm therm}$ is read
off as the first crossing at which the bias against the exact value falls inside
two standard errors and stays there (\sect{relaxation-correction}), so the
closed form is the criterion for equilibration and not only the measure of it;
every verdict of equilibration in \fig{money} depends on it. In four dimensions this
criterion would have to be replaced, for instance by requiring agreement between
chains initialized from different distributions, by a plateau criterion, or by
comparison with a long reference run. The construction itself is unaffected,
since the sampler never makes use of the exact value.

The division of labor is the part of this method we expect to outlast the
particular architecture used here, and \sect{u2obs} shows where the line falls.
A lifted configuration arrives with its topological sector already correct and
its short-distance structure still several standard errors out; HMC repairs the
second in tens of trajectories at the couplings where it repairs it at all, and
in none at the stiffest rung we tried, while under plain HMC it does not reach
the first within any budget we can afford. What the model contributes is therefore the sector, supplied by
transport rather than by sampling, and not the local structure. The same asymmetry has been
reported independently in two-dimensional $\phi^4$ theory, where a brief
rethermalization was found to remove a short-distance mismatch quickly and a
mismatch in a relevant direction slowly \cite{hasenfratz2026}, which suggests
that where the boundary between the two falls is a property of the blocking step
rather than of the theory or of the generative architecture. Normalizing flows
\cite{albergo2019,hasenfratz2026cascade}, gauge-equivariant L-CNNs
\cite{favoni2020} and other diffusion architectures
\cite{zhu2024,zhu2025,aarts2026,komijani2026} are natural candidates
for extending it to more complicated theories.

\section{Network, training and sampler settings}
\label{app:settings}

The U(1) network has $324{,}954$ parameters at
hidden width $56$ and depth $4$; the U(2) one has $403{,}394$ at width $64$ and
the same depth; both use $3\times3$ kernels. Training is denoising score
matching under Adam, for $100$ epochs at batch size $16$ and learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$ in U(1) and $120$ epochs at batch size $32$ and
$2\times10^{-4}$ in U(2), holding out a tenth of each training set for
validation; the deployed U(2) run stopped at epoch $109$ rather than $120$, and
both checkpoints hold the exponential moving average of the weights at their
best validation epoch. The U(1) objective carries one additional term, a soft topological-charge
penalty on the one-step denoised estimate at weight $0.1$; the U(2) objective is
denoising score matching alone. The symmetry augmentation of
\sect{scoring-network} applies a uniformly random element of the $16$-element
rotation, reflection and charge-conjugation group, before blocking and noising,
to a randomly chosen half of the mini-batches, one element shared across the
batch. The network's inputs are gauge-invariant channels: six from the noisy
fine field, the cosine and sine of its plaquette angle and of the two $1\times2$
rectangle angles, and five from the coarse field, the cosine and sine of its
plaquette and $2\times2$ loop angles together with the wrapped plaquette angle
itself, which a cosine and sine pair cannot distinguish from its negative and
which carries the sign of the local charge density. The
deployed U(2) checkpoint is trained on eleven fixed $(L,\beta)$ rungs spanning
nine distinct couplings, and the deployed U(1) one on four fixed rungs plus $78$
couplings drawn at random below its ceiling; \sect{coverage} varies exactly this list and
nothing else. A trajectory means one integration followed by one
accept--reject, counted whether or not it is accepted. Above a reference
coupling the integrator step is scaled as $\beta^{-1/2}$ and the number of steps
inversely, holding the trajectory length near unity; rounding the step count to
an integer leaves that length within a few percent of one rather than exactly
fixed. The scaling carries no volume term, so acceptance falls as the lattice
grows at fixed trajectory length: on the deployed rungs it is $0.80$ at
$L=16$, $0.69$ at $L=32$ and $0.37$ at $L=64$. A trajectory is therefore not a fixed
amount of work across the ladder: in U(2) it is $9$ integration
steps at $\beta=28$ and $72$ at $\beta=1660$, and U(1), whose reference coupling
is lower, uses $13$ and $102$ at the same two. Improvement factors compare two
arms at one coupling and are unaffected, but trajectory counts should not be
compared between couplings without that factor.

Sampling uses $200$ ancestral steps, each followed by one
Langevin corrector step, with the charge projection applied every ten steps
below $\sigma=0.5$. The noise schedule is geometric between a maximum of $6$ and
a coupling-dependent floor $\min(0.03,\,c/\sqrt{\beta})$. In U(1) the deployed
sampler uses $c=0.1$ where training used $c=0.3$, so at the two stiffer ladder
rungs it finishes below any noise level the network was trained
at.\footnote{The training floor is $0.03$ at every U(1) rung, since
$0.3/\sqrt{\beta}$ exceeds it below $\beta=100$ and the training set stops at
$57$. Deployed, the floor is $0.0266$ at $\beta=14.15$ and $0.0135$ at
$\beta=55.02$, so $5$ and $27$ of the $200$ steps respectively run below it.
This is tolerable because the floor is one tenth of the crossover scale
$\sigma_c=1/\sqrt{\beta}$ of the analytic blend above: at the terminal noise
level the blend weight is $0.88$ and $0.98$ at those two rungs, so the drift
there is almost entirely the exact Wilson score and the learned part is
correspondingly small. U(2) deploys no such override, and its blend is off, so
its sampler never leaves the trained range of $\sigma$.} In U(2) the determinant sector is followed by $30$
conditional SU(2) sweeps, each a heatbath pass followed by two microcanonical
overrelaxation passes, which generate the SU(2) sector at fixed $\psi$; the
marginal winding move of \sect{instanton_updates_classical} resamples with $25$
such sweeps. No local update is applied to the determinant sector in either
theory.

The training ensembles themselves are burned in once per rung, at a cost no
deployed chain could afford: $200$, $600$ or $2000$ HMC trajectories on the
fixed U(1) rungs and $200$ on the randomly placed ones, and in U(2) sixty
heatbath and microcanonical
overrelaxation \cite{creutz1987} sweeps followed by $150$ to $400$
trajectories.

\section{Sector frequencies in the training data}
\label{app:sectors}

Of the eleven rungs the deployed U(2) checkpoint is trained on, the five below
$\beta=51.75$ are sampled by HMC, and the remaining six are both augmented and
started from sectors drawn from the exact $P(Q)$ at that coupling, the SU(2)
part being regenerated afterwards by the conditional heatbath, which leaves
$\psi$ and $Q$ untouched. Whether those frequencies have to be correct is a
question about what the network must learn, and it can be settled directly.

We built a second training set differing in one respect: its charges were drawn
uniformly over the sectors the exact distribution supports rather than with its
weights, so coverage is identical and the frequencies are deliberately wrong.
The two sets differ by a median factor of $5.6$ in $\langle Q^2\rangle$.
Training two networks identically on them and running the full ladder from each
gives a mean $|z|$ against the closed form of $0.924$ against $0.912$ at $L=32$
and $0.594$ against $0.690$ at $L=64$, differences of $0.012$ and $0.096$.

Those differences bound the effect weakly rather than tightly. The $41$ scored
observables are Wilson loops of different sizes on the same configurations and
are strongly correlated: the participation ratio of their correlation matrix is
$3.7$ at $L=32$ and $3.2$ at $L=64$, so the mean of $|z|$ over them carries the
sampling error of fewer than four independent draws rather than of $41$. The
standard error on the difference between two
scorecards is then $0.44$ and $0.47$, so the differences amount to $0.03$ and
$0.20$ standard errors, and
the smallest either comparison could resolve is about $0.9$ --- larger than
the half-normal value $\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.798$ that a correct model with correct
error bars would itself score. The comparison therefore excludes a large
dependence on sector frequencies and not a small one.

The construction does not rest on that null alone, since augmentation already
supplies charged configurations from a fixed instanton shift that consults
nothing about the theory. What the exact $P(Q)$ cannot be replaced for here is
scoring: it is the reference every deviation in this paper is measured against,
and \sect{conclusions} discusses what would take its place where no closed form
exists.

\section{The analytic-score blend in U(2)}
\label{app:blend}

The analytic object the blend of \sect{reverse_diffusion} would contribute in
U(2) is the score of the determinant-sector marginal $w_{\det}$ of \Eq{wdet}. Probing it on the lift at $L=32$, at a
coarse coupling of $\beta_c=67.41$ and so a fine coupling of
$\beta_f=263.50$,\footnote{These probes are their own lifts rather than points
of the grid of \app{grid}, whose neighboring point runs from $\beta_c=67.59$ to
$\beta_f=264.2$.} the plaquette's relative deviation runs
$-6\times10^{-4}$, $-0.19$ and $-0.30$ at blend weights $0$, $0.5$ and $1$,
while the transported charge is destroyed at full weight: $\langle Q^2\rangle$
runs $0.45$, $0.47$ and $10.7$ against an exact $0.39$. The pattern repeats at
every coupling probed, including one lying on a training rung.

Why the U(1) construction does not carry over is not settled. One candidate is
that the noise-smeared coupling it uses is derived from the harmonic limit of a
Wilson weight, and $w_{\det}$ is not of Wilson form, so the smearing is applied
to a weight it was not constructed for. A second is that the lift needs the
conditional $p(\psi_f\mid\psi_c)$ while the analytic score is the $\psi$
marginal, and the two cannot be mixed at a fixed weight. We report the
measurement and leave the mechanism open.

\section{Exact observables in 2D U(1) and U(2)}
\label{app:exact}

\tb{u1-exact-observables} and \tb{u2-exact-observables} collect the closed forms
used for scoring, with the sources they follow.

The U(2) score network is conditioned on a matched U(1) coupling $\beta_1$
rather than on $\beta$, because the determinant-sector weight $w_{\det}$ of
\Eq{wdet} is not of Wilson form. We take $\beta_1$ to be the Wilson coupling
whose single-plaquette weight is closest to $w_{\det}$ under the minimum
Kullback--Leibler projection onto the Wilson family \cite{amarinagaoka2000}.
The Wilson weight is a von Mises distribution in $\alpha$ with concentration
$\beta_1$, so the projection reduces to the standard moment condition for that
family \cite{mardiajupp2000},
\begin{equation}
    \frac{I_1(\beta_1)}{I_0(\beta_1)} = \langle\cos\alpha\rangle_{w_{\det}}.
    \label{eq:vonmises}
\end{equation}
Along the deployed ladder the raw couplings $\beta=28$, $105.65$, $416.52$ and
$1660.08$ give $\beta_1=7.02$, $26.42$, $104.13$ and $415.02$. This projection
relates weights on one lattice and is unrelated to the ladder matching of
\Eq{topology-matching}, which relates two lattices.

\section{Topology-matched ladder couplings}
\label{app:matching}

\Eq{topology-matching} has no closed-form solution, so $\beta_f$ is obtained
numerically: $\langle Q^2\rangle$ is evaluated from the character expansion of
\app{exact} at fixed $L$, and the root is bracketed between $\beta_c$ and
$64(\beta_c+1)$ and taken by Brent's method to a tolerance of $10^{-9}$. The
bracket is safe because $\langle Q^2\rangle$ at fixed $L$ decreases
monotonically in $\beta$, and the left end is below the solution for every step
of the form $L_f=2L_c$.

Iterated from the deployed base at $L=16$, $\beta=28$, where the exact
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ is $1.00119$, this gives $\beta_f=105.651$ at $L=32$ and
$416.524$ at $L=64$, holding $\langle Q^2\rangle$ fixed to the five digits
shown.

\section{The coupling grid}
\label{app:grid}

Every checkpoint in \sect{coverage} is scored on the same grid, and each point of
it is an independent lift: a coarse ensemble at $\beta_c$ is lifted to
$\beta_f$ at twice the linear size, and no charge is carried between points.
\tb{grid} lists them. The grid holds $L$ fixed and raises $\beta$, so it is not
a ladder and the invariance of \Eq{topology-matching} does not hold along it;
\sect{coverage} gives the consequence.

The budget shortens as the coupling rises, since the runs at the top of the grid
exist to establish that no classical arm equilibrates rather than to measure how
quickly one does. Each lower bound in \sect{cost} is quoted against the budget
of the coupling that produced it.

\section{Statistical methodology}
\label{app:stats}

Integrated autocorrelation times are computed as specified in
\cite{madrassokal1988,wolff2004}, including Sokal's automatic windowing, and
$t_{\rm interval}=2\tau_{\rm int}$. The standard error on a mean over $N$
correlated measurements is then
$\sigma_{\rm mean}^2 = 2\tau_{\rm int}\,\sigma^2/N$, with $\sigma^2$ the
variance across measurements, and every deviation quoted in units of a standard
error uses that $\tau_{\rm int}$-aware form. The per-configuration spread
$\sigma_{\rm cfg}$ of \sect{distributions} is instead the plain standard
deviation of a Wilson loop across the individual configurations of an ensemble,
carrying no $\tau_{\rm int}$ factor because it is a width rather than an error
on a mean.

The equilibration time is read off the data as a crossing: $t_{\rm therm}$ is
the first trajectory after which $|\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_t-\mathcal{O}_{\rm
exact}|$ stays within two standard errors for five consecutive measurements,
maximized over the plaquette, $W(2\times2)$ and $W(4\times4)$. For
$t_{\rm interval}$ this is the usual
statement about a noisy estimator. For $t_{\rm therm}$ it carries more weight,
because a first-crossing index is set by the last chain to settle, so its
dominant uncertainty is which chains the ensemble happens to contain, and a
resample in which the crossing never occurs is recorded as such rather than
being dropped. The resulting interval is asymmetric wherever an arm is already
at the floor, equilibrating within the first record: resampling which chains
contribute can only delay that crossing and never bring it earlier, so the upper
edge reports how early a resampled set of chains fails the five-record rule
rather than giving a second estimate of the equilibration time.

Because that band is set by $\sigma_{\rm cfg}$, a fixed relative bias becomes
harder to clear as the volume grows, and along the ladder this is what governs
$t_{\rm therm}$ rather than any change in the map. \tb{band_scaling} gives the
two scans. In the U(1) volume scan, which holds $\beta$ fixed so that only the
volume changes, the lift's deviation before any trajectory is flat at $869$,
$892$ and $820$~ppm while $\sigma_{\rm cfg}$ halves at each doubling, the
measured ratios being $0.494$ and $0.493$ against the $0.500$ of $V^{-1/2}$: the
map is volume-independent, as a fully convolutional network conditioned on the
coupling should be, and $t_{\rm therm}$ grows from $9$ to $52$ purely because
the criterion tightens.

Along the U(2) ladder both the volume and the coupling rise, so $\sigma_{\rm
cfg}$ falls eight- to ninefold per rung. The lift's residual deviation after $400$
trajectories is $+10.1$, $+9.2$ and $+5.7$~ppm while the
band runs $\pm101$, $\pm12.7$ and $\pm3.0$~ppm: the residual falls by under a
factor of two across the three rungs and the band by a factor of $34$. At the
first rung that residual is a tenth of the band and the arm equilibrates in five
trajectories; at the third it is twice the band and the arm does not equilibrate
within the budget. The top rung therefore does not show the construction failing at large
volume, where its starting deviation is in fact smallest; it shows a residual
that is present at every rung becoming resolvable once the statistical
resolution is sharp enough to see it.

The resampling unit is the chain. Every error bar on a sampled ensemble quantity
resamples whole chains. Beyond the usual underestimate for an autocorrelated
series \cite{wolff2004}, the case here fails outright: a topologically frozen
chain has zero within-chain variance in $Q$, so pooling over configurations
reports a vanishing error on an arm that has sampled nothing. A generated
ensemble is the exception, since a lift's configurations descend from
independent base chains and are themselves the independent unit; there the
configuration-level bootstrap and the naive standard error agree closely, and
the generated-ensemble error bars reported here are the bootstrap ones.

\section{Cost of building a lifted configuration}
\label{app:overhead}

The cost that \Eq{improvement-factor} does not count is producing the
lifted configuration: a base ensemble at the bottom of the ladder, plus
one reverse diffusion per lift. \sect{cost} gives its size, which on this
hardware is not small. It is dominated by the reverse-diffusion sampler, whose
step count is set for accuracy rather than speed and is the obvious place to
look for a reduction.

Two features of that overhead are what keep the trajectory comparison
meaningful. The base ensemble is generated once, at a coupling where sampling is
cheap, and is amortized over every configuration lifted from it; and the reverse
diffusion is a fixed number of network evaluations, the same at every coupling,
while $t_{\rm interval}$ in the numerator grows without bound as the classical
chain freezes. A fixed overhead cannot close a diverging gap, and above
$\beta\approx90$, where no classical arm equilibrates at all, there is no finite
quantity for it to close.
