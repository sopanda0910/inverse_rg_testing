\title{Diffusion models as preconditioners for hybrid Monte Carlo in
lattice gauge theory}

\author{Shivam Panda}
% \affiliation{}
\author{Jinchen He}
% \affiliation{}
\author{Yong Zhao}

\begin{abstract}
Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling of lattice gauge fields suffers from critical
slowing down, and its topological charge can freeze entirely at small lattice
spacing. We show that a diffusion model used as a preconditioner can bypass
topological freezing: the chain is handed a starting configuration whose
topology is approximately correct, so that it rethermalizes in a small number of
trajectories. The diffusion model performs an inverse renormalization-group
transformation, upsampling a coarse configuration to a fine one, and its output
preconditions the HMC chain in place of a cold or hot start. The topological
charge is transported from the coarse configuration rather than generated from
scratch, so that it requires only small corrections. The network is fully
convolutional and conditioned on the coupling rather than on the lattice size,
so a single trained model serves every volume, and we find that
per-configuration accuracy does not degrade at four times the volume.
\SP{\sout{Volume scaling (and the fact that this method is convolutional and theoretically should be independent of volume) should be mentioned earlier, not in a sentence that is only related to training coverage}}
In 2D U(1) and U(2), the method delivers configurations at
$\beta \approx 1660$ on a $128\times128$ lattice, eight times the linear size of
the $16\times16$ ensemble from which the ladder starts. After $400$ trajectories
the delivered ensemble agrees with the exact closed form to $1$ part per million
on the plaquette, against $51$ parts per million for a cold start over the same
budget. \SP{\sout{Topological charge comparison results should be in a separate sentence}} The
topological charge separates the two more sharply still: the cold chain does not
change sector once in that budget, while the preconditioned ensembles carry the
exact charge variance to within a few percent. Measured in trajectories per
independent configuration, the preconditioned chain is unboundedly cheaper than
plain HMC above $\beta \approx 25$, where HMC supplies no independent
configurations at any budget, and against HMC with an exact winding update it is
break-even below $\beta \approx 90$ and cheaper by a median factor of at least
five above it, where that baseline ceases to equilibrate. Widening the trained
range from $\beta \approx 416$ to $\beta \approx 2000$ carries the method from
failing above its former ceiling to working at every coupling tested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Lattice Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) \JH{\sout{discretizes the strong interaction
non-perturbatively.} provides a non-perturbative description of the strong interaction in a discretized setup of space-time. }
\JH{\sout{Configurations, 4D grids carrying an SU(3) matrix on
every edge, together with a dynamical fermion field, are sampled from the
Boltzmann distribution $p(\phi)\propto e^{-S[\phi]}$ in the lattice action $S$,
and observables are Monte Carlo estimates over that ensemble.}
In lattice QCD calculations, the path integral is replaced by sample averaging over measurements on Euclidean configurations, which are usually generated from Markov chain Monte carlo according to the Boltzmann distribution $p(\phi)\propto e^{-S[\phi]}$ with a given lattice action $S$. }

The cost of Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) grows close to exponentially as the lattice
spacing shrinks, a phenomenon known as critical slowing down
\cite{schaefer2011}; and generation separately suffers from topological
freezing, where large action barriers leave
charge sectors unexplored \cite{luscher2011}, so a batch of configurations no
longer follows the expected distribution of topological charge
(\sect{freezing}).

An HMC chain is conventionally initialized from a cold or a hot configuration.
A cold start sets every link to the identity; a hot start draws each link
independently from the Haar measure on the gauge group. Neither carries any
information about the target, so a chain begun from either must run for a
thermalization time $t_{\rm therm}$ before its samples are usable. We show that
$t_{\rm therm}$ can be reduced from hundreds of trajectories to a few, or to
zero, by using a diffusion model as a preconditioner instead. The model inverts
one renormalization-group (RG) step, generating a configuration on the finer of
two matched lattices conditioned on its coarse partner, and iterating that step
reaches lattices and couplings at which direct sampling has stalled.

Full QCD being expensive, we work in 2D U(1) and 2D U(2), which retain the
features that matter and are exactly solvable, so that every output can be
scored against a closed form rather than against another simulation
(\sect{setup}).

Machine learning has been applied to this problem largely through normalizing
flows \cite{albergo2019} and diffusion models: Wang, Aarts and Zhou cast the
reverse diffusion as a stochastic-quantization scheme for lattice field theory
\cite{wang2024}, Zhu et al.\ applied one to 2D U(1) configurations
\cite{zhu2024,zhu2025}, conditioning on the coupling so that one model covers a
range of them, and Aarts et al.\ generalized to non-abelian 2D U(2)/SU(2) with
an equivariant architecture \cite{aarts2026}. In each of these the learned model
is the sampler: the ensemble it produces is the result, and correctness is
either taken from the model directly or restored by an accept/reject step
wrapped around it, as in the Metropolis-adjusted annealed Langevin sampler of
\cite{aarts2026}. Either way the burden falls on the model at the target
volume, and \cite{zhu2025,aarts2026} both report that it transfers to larger
couplings and lattice sizes. A separate line of work
starts HMC from a constructed configuration instead: Detmold and Endres
\cite{detmoldendres2015,detmoldendres2016} built a renormalization-group-matched
coarse-to-fine map, started HMC from its output, and measured rethermalization
against cold and hot starts, for pure Yang--Mills and then with fermions.

This paper is organized as follows: \sect{setup} fixes notation and defines the classical baselines, \sect{method}
gives the method, \sect{seedquality} and \sect{coverage} report what the
preconditioner is worth and what training must cover, and \sect{conclusions}
asks what carries over to full QCD.

We work in two dimensions with two gauge groups, chosen so that the cost of
producing independent, correctly distributed configurations is dominated by the
same obstruction that dominates it in four-dimensional QCD, while the correct
answer remains known in closed form.

The first is compact U(1), the pure-gauge sector of quantum electrodynamics in
two dimensions, which is the standard first testbed for generative models of
gauge fields \cite{albergo2019,zhu2025}. A gauge link carries a group element on
each edge of the lattice; for U(1) the group element is a phase,
$U_{x,\mu}=e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}$ with $\theta_{x,\mu}\in(-\pi,\pi]$, one angle
per edge. Compactness of that angle is what gives the theory a
topological charge and a sector structure, which is the feature it shares with
four-dimensional QCD and the reason the theory is used here. We follow the
conventions of \cite{gattringerlang2010} throughout.

The second is U(2), which is non-abelian and is the extension the method is
tested on. Its group structure, $\mathrm{U}(2)=(\mathrm{U}(1)\times
\mathrm{SU}(2))/\mathbb{Z}_2$, is the same product form as the electroweak gauge
group $\mathrm{SU}(2)_L\times \mathrm{U}(1)_Y$, though the analogy is structural
rather than physical, the electroweak theory being chiral and four-dimensional.
What matters for the method is that the whole topological charge sits in the
abelian determinant factor while the dynamics remain genuinely non-abelian. A
U(2) link is a unitary $2\times2$ matrix, which for computational convenience we
store in the split representation
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,\mu} = e^{i\theta_{x,\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}
q_1 + i q_4 & q_3 + i q_2 \\
-q_3 + i q_2 & q_1 - i q_4
\end{pmatrix},
    \label{eq:u2-link}
\end{equation}
an SU(2) element written as a unit quaternion ($\sum_a q_a^2 = 1$) times a
central phase, so five real scalars describe a U(2) link.

Every observable is built from the plaquette
\begin{equation}
    U_{x,p} \equiv U_{x,\mu} U_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} U_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu}^\dagger U_{x,\nu}^\dagger,
    \label{eq:plaquette}
\end{equation}
the smallest closed loop and hence gauge invariant; products of adjacent
plaquettes form the larger Wilson loops used as observables. In U(1) the
plaquette reduces to a signed sum of link angles,
\begin{equation}
    \theta_{x,p} \equiv \theta_{x,\mu} + \theta_{x+\hat{\mu},\nu} - \theta_{x+\hat{\nu},\mu} - \theta_{x,\nu},
    \label{eq:theta-plaquette}
\end{equation}
whose order is immaterial; in U(2) the matrix ordering matters. The Wilson
actions,
\begin{align}
    S_{U(1)} &= -\beta \sum_{x}\cos(\theta_{x,p}), \label{eq:action-u1}\\
    S_{U(2)} &= -\beta \sum_{x} \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Tr}(U_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:action-u2}
\end{align}
are gauge invariant since they depend only on plaquettes.

\subsection{Topological Freezing}
\label{sec:freezing}

The topological charge is the wrapped plaquette sum,
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{x} \mathrm{wrap}\,(\theta_{x,p}),
    \label{eq:q-u1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{wrap}(\alpha) \equiv \mathrm{atan2}(\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha)$
returns the representative in $(-\pi,\pi]$, the two-argument arctangent being
what resolves the quadrant.

For U(2), $Q$ is defined by the same formula applied to the determinant plaquette
phase, $\alpha_x = \mathrm{wrap}(\arg\det U_{x,p})$. The central phases in
\Eq{plaquette} are scalars, so they factor out of the product and combine into
exactly the U(1) plaquette sum of \Eq{theta-plaquette}; the SU(2) factors have
unit determinant and drop out of it entirely. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \det(U_{x,p})=e^{2i\theta_{x,p}}.
    \label{eq:det-plaquette}
\end{equation}
\SP{\sout{Not sure if we really need to get into so much detail in terms of simply connected or multiply connected}}
The SU(2) component therefore contributes nothing to $Q$, and cannot: it carries
no topological charge in two dimensions. We call the U(1) component of a U(2) link
its determinant sector, $\psi \equiv \arg\det U = \mathrm{wrap}(2\theta)$.
The determinant phase advances twice as fast as the
central phase $\theta$ does. A configuration whose links are purely central
therefore carries twice the charge the same phases would carry in U(1), which is
why the U(1) instanton moves $Q$ by two rather than by one when it is
transferred to U(2) (\sect{instanton_updates_classical}).

Since $Q$ is defined by the wrapped sum of plaquettes, it can only change if some
$\theta_{x,p}$ is pushed past $\pm\pi$. At large $\beta$ every plaquette angle
clusters near zero due to the Wilson actions in \Eq{action-u1}.
Changing $Q$ through purely local updates therefore requires passing through an
intermediate configuration with some $\theta_{x,p}$ near $\pm\pi$, which costs
$\Delta S = 2\beta$ and is accepted with probability $\sim e^{-2\beta}$. That exponential
suppression is the root cause of topological freezing, and it worsens toward the
continuum limit as $\beta$ grows. By \Eq{det-plaquette} the mechanism
is identical in U(2).

\subsection{Classical Baseline}
\label{sec:instanton_updates_classical}

Configurations are generated by HMC \cite{duane1987,neal2011}, discretized by
the Omelyan integrator \cite{omelyan2003} for U(1) and by an exponential-map
leapfrog for the group-valued U(2) fields. The whole trajectory is offered as a
single Metropolis proposal, which is what guarantees sampling from the Boltzmann
distribution despite the integrator's discretization error.

In 2D U(1), winding HMC \cite{albandea2021} proposes an instanton field, a
field carrying exactly one unit of topological charge, as a global update
accepted by the same Metropolis criterion. It delays the freezing barrier to
much higher $\beta$ than standard HMC without eliminating it
(\tb{u1_frozen}), and being the stronger comparison it is the
classical baseline throughout this paper.

\label{sec:instanton}%
In 2D U(2) an analogous construction is available because $Q$ depends only on
$\psi$. The SU(2) sector integrates out of the action exactly, plaquette by
plaquette against Haar measure, which is a 2D solvability statement with no
analogue in $D>2$ or with fermions. That integral gives the closed-form marginal
weight $w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta)=2I_1(z)/z$, $z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2)$
(\sect{u2-extension}).

The U(1) instanton transfers to U(2), but only halfway. A purely central phase shift
$\theta\to\theta+\lambda$ commutes with the SU(2) sector entirely, so it can be
applied without disturbing it, at the same cost $\Delta S = 2\pi^2\beta/V$ as in
U(1).
But the determinant sector is $\psi=2\theta$, so the shift moves $\psi$ by
$2\lambda$ and $Q$ by exactly two. Every proposal changes $Q$ by two, so it
cannot change the parity of $Q$, meaning whether $Q$ is even or odd: a chain
under this move stays for its whole history in the parity class it started in.
We call it the even-only move, and a move that crosses between the two classes a
parity flip.

Reaching an odd sector from an even one takes three steps: (i) propose
$\psi'=\psi\pm\lambda$, changing $Q$ by $\pm1$; (ii) accept with the Metropolis
ratio of the marginal action alone, $\min(1,e^{-\Delta S_{\det}})$, leaving the
SU(2) sector out of the decision because it is about to be replaced; and (iii)
resample $q\sim p(q\mid\psi')$ exactly by Creutz--Kennedy--Pendleton heatbath
\cite{creutz1980,kennedypendleton1985}. We call this the marginal move.

At $L=64$ and $\beta=416.524$, $200$ cold-started trajectories
over $64$ chains give zero accepted parity flips under the even-only move, so the
measured odd-sector weight is $P(\mathrm{odd})=0$ against an exact $0.4929$.
Under the marginal move the same budget gives $7661$ flips and
$P(\mathrm{odd})=0.4939$. \tb{classical} repeats the
comparison at $L=16$ across three couplings. The comparison is repeated at
$L=16$ across three couplings in \app{tables}.

Two directions in the $(L,\beta)$ plane must be kept apart here, because $P(Q)$
behaves oppositely along them, and only one of them is a continuum limit.
Raising $\beta$ at fixed $L$ shrinks the physical volume, since
$\beta=1/(e^2a^2)$ fixes the spacing and the physical extent is $La$. The charge
distribution collapses with it: in 2D U(1) at $L=32$ the exact
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ falls from $7.74$ to $0.014$ as $\beta$ runs from $4$ to
$256$. A theory examined along that direction does become topologically trivial,
and carrying a charge along it unchanged would be wrong.

The ladder moves along the other direction. Doubling $L$ while quadrupling
$\beta$ holds $La$ fixed, so it is a continuum limit at fixed physical volume,
and there $\langle Q^2\rangle$ tends to $e^2V_{\rm phys}/4\pi^2$, a finite
physical quantity. Taken out of the deployed U(2) base at $L=16$, $\beta=28$, the
exact determinant-sector value runs $1.00119$, $0.94338$, $0.93053$, $0.92740$
and $0.92662$ over five steps, converging rather than vanishing. Topology is not
trivial in this limit; what the earlier direction removes is the volume, not the
topology. Transporting the charge is therefore the correct operation along the
ladder, and $P(Q)$ is the same distribution at every rung up to the
lattice-spacing corrections visible in that sequence.

Those corrections are also what fixes the matching criterion. Because
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ approaches a physical constant, requiring it to be equal at
successive rungs, as \Eq{topology-matching} does, is a way of holding the
physical volume fixed with the topological susceptibility itself as the
yardstick, and it removes the residual drift that the tree-level rule
$\beta_f=4\beta_c$ leaves behind. The choice does not change what limit is being
taken. Iterated from the same base, both criteria give $\beta_f/\beta_c\to4$,
$3.77$, $3.94$, $3.99$, $4.00$ for \Eq{topology-matching} and $3.86$, $3.96$,
$3.99$, $4.00$ for the character condition, so each is a continuum limit at
fixed physical volume. They differ in which volume: in units of $1/e^2$ the two
trajectories settle at $L^2/\beta_1=39.5$ and $38.4$, a difference of $3\%$ that
is the usual scale-setting ambiguity at finite spacing and vanishes with it.
What \Eq{topology-matching} adds is that $\langle Q^2\rangle$ is constant along
the way rather than only in the limit, which is what a transported charge
requires. The coarse ensemble's own distance from the
continuum remains, and it is the one systematic the construction inherits rather
than creates: a finer base would carry a smaller one. \sect{coverage} evaluates
the fixed-$L$ direction instead, one independent lift per coupling, and never
carries a charge across it.

\subsection{Training Data}
\label{sec:naive-blocking}

Where the baselines of \sect{setup} repair topology inside a running chain, the
method here acts once, before the chain starts. It is one inverse
renormalization-group step: a coarse configuration at
$L_c\times L_c$ and coupling $\beta_c$ is lifted to a fine one at $L_f=2L_c$ and
$\beta_f\approx4\beta_c$, with the fine topological charge transported exactly
from the coarse configuration and only the remaining ultraviolet structure
supplied by the model. We call one such $(L,\beta)$ point a \emph{rung}, and
the sequence of rungs running from the base ensemble up to the target the
\emph{ladder}.

A fine configuration is generated at a $\beta$ small enough that HMC with winding updates has a finite decorrelation
time even for the slow modes. Its coarse partner is fixed completely by summing the neighboring links,
\begin{align*}
\theta_x'(X,Y) &= \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_x(2X,2Y) + \theta_x(2X+1,2Y)\big),\\
\theta_y'(X,Y) &= \mathrm{wrap}\big(\theta_y(2X,2Y) + \theta_y(2X,2Y+1)\big),
\end{align*}
with $\theta'$ the coarse link angle. This makes the coarse plaquette angle equal
the wrapped sum of its four constituent fine sub-plaquettes exactly, and hence
$Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} = Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ up to wrap events, which are
exponentially suppressed at the couplings where topology is the obstruction:
they occur in $0.3\%$ of the $4096$ configurations of the U(2) base ensemble.
The pipeline does not rely on the identity in any case, since at deployment the
charge is imposed rather than inherited through blocking.

A standard inverse renormalization-group step fixes the blocked coupling by
requiring that the two lattices agree on a chosen observable \cite{swendsen1979},
and the usual choice is the plaquette, equivalently the first character
coefficient $r_1(\beta)=I_1(\beta)/I_0(\beta)$. A coarse plaquette is built from
four fine ones, so blocking maps it as $r_1(\beta_c)=r_1(\beta_f)^4$
\cite{migdal1975,drouffezuber1983}; this is the minimum Kullback--Leibler
projection onto the Wilson family \cite{amarinagaoka2000} and reduces to the
tree-level $\beta_c\approx\beta_f/4$ through $I_1/I_0\approx1-1/(2\beta)$.

We match a different observable, and the reason is specific to this pipeline.
How many sectors an ensemble populates is measured by $\chi_t V$, the
topological susceptibility times the volume, which is $\langle Q^2\rangle$
itself \cite{bonatirossi2019}. Sector transport is an identity, so whatever
$P(Q)$ the base ensemble carries is what every rung above it receives, and a
drift in $\langle Q^2\rangle$ is the one error the pipeline has no mechanism to
remove; a mismatch in the plaquette, by contrast, is repaired by the
rethermalization sweeps of \sect{reverse_diffusion}. We therefore fix $\beta_f$ at
each step as the root of
\begin{equation}
    \langle Q^2\rangle_{\beta_f,\,L_f} \;=\; \langle Q^2\rangle_{\beta_c,\,L_c},
    \qquad L_f = 2L_c,
    \label{eq:topology-matching}
\end{equation}
with both sides evaluated in the closed form of \app{exact}. A solution exists
near the character-matched coupling because to leading order
$\chi_t V\approx V/(4\pi^2\beta)$, so doubling $L$ quadruples $V$ while the
matched coupling quadruples $\beta$; \app{matching} gives the numerical solution
and the resulting schedules.

At high $\beta$ the fine ensemble used to build training pairs is itself frozen
in $Q$, with $\langle Q^2\rangle\ll1$, so HMC returns configurations almost
entirely at $Q=0$. A training set built purely from HMC output at a fixed high
coupling would then never show the network the local structure of a
configuration at nonzero $Q$ at that coupling. That structure is what the
network requires, since at deployment a coarse input may carry any $Q$ and the
transport step imposes it regardless.

We manufacture the missing coverage rather than sampling it, by two mechanisms
that we distinguish because only one of them needs the theory to be solvable.
Sector augmentation takes a fixed fraction of the fine ensemble at a rung, draws
a charge shift uniformly from a small integer range, adds that many copies of the
instanton field (\sect{instanton}), and relaxes the result locally at fixed
topology to remove the ultraviolet error the instanton leaves behind. It
consults no closed form. The alternative is to draw a configuration's charge
from the exact $P(Q)$ and impose it, which does.

\subsection{Score network}
\label{sec:scoring-network}

\begin{figure*}[bt]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{u1_2d/45_architecture.png}
    \caption{The gauge-covariant score network, shown for U(1), with
  $324{,}954$ parameters in $4$ FiLM blocks of width $56$. The U(2) network has
  the same structure, with width $64$ and $403{,}394$ parameters. The network
  outputs one number per plaquette and turns these into a score with a lattice
  curl, which can represent the gradient of any sum of functions of plaquettes,
  so this form loses no generality. No part of the network depends on the
  lattice size $L$, because the convolutions are circular and each site is
  normalized on its own. One trained network can therefore be used at every
  lattice size on the ladder.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

Diffusion models generate by reversing a noising process. A forward It\^o SDE
$dx = f(x,t)dt + g(t)dW$ carries the data distribution to a tractable reference
over $t\in[0,T]$. By Anderson's theorem \cite{anderson1982} it has a
reverse-time companion with the same marginals, whose only unknown is the score
$\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$; denoising score matching \cite{vincent2011} estimates
that score by regressing on the known conditional transition kernel, and Song et
al.\ \cite{song2021} supply the discretizations used here. The standard
construction assumes $x\in\mathbb R^n$ with a Gaussian kernel. Neither holds for
link angles, which live on a compact periodic domain, so both the forward
process and the score it induces are built from the domain's own kernel.

Each configuration is a vector $\vec\theta$ with $2L^2$ components, noised
independently with the wrapped Gaussian transition kernel
$$K(\theta;\hat\theta,\sigma_t)=\frac{1}{\sigma_t\sqrt{2\pi}} \sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(\theta-\hat{\theta} + 2\pi r)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right),$$
with $\hat\theta$ the clean angle. As $\sigma_t$ grows this approaches the uniform
distribution on the circle, and the regression target is $\nabla_\theta\log K$.

The architecture is shown in \fig{architecture}. The network sees two
fields, the noisy fine configuration and the coarse configuration conditioning
it, each as gauge-invariant channels. Because the two live on grids of different
size, the coarse field is first enlarged to the fine grid by nearest-neighbour
replication, each coarse value copied onto all four sites of its block, after
which the two are concatenated channel-wise. The noise level $\sigma_t$ and
coupling $\beta$ enter through a sinusoidal embedding feeding per-block
Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018}, into which the
spatial mean of the coarse conditioning channels is also projected. That gives
every block direct access to a genuinely global quantity, since the mean wrapped
coarse plaquette angle equals $2\pi Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}/V$ by definition
of $Q$, and a convolutional network with bounded kernel size cannot otherwise
reconstruct a lattice-wide sum.

Gauge invariance is built into the architecture, by the construction given
below. The Wilson action's $90^\circ$-rotation, reflection and
charge-conjugation symmetries are not. Training instead applies a uniformly
random element of that $16$-element group, before blocking and noising, to a
randomly selected half of the mini-batches, one element shared across the
batch.

The network predicts one scalar $h_p$ per plaquette rather than a link-valued
score, and the score is assembled by the lattice curl,
\begin{align}
  s_x(x,y) &= h_p(x,y) - h_p(x,y-1), \label{eq:curl-x}\\
  s_y(x,y) &= h_p(x-1,y) - h_p(x,y). \label{eq:curl-y}
\end{align}
This is the general form of the gradient of any sum of functions of plaquettes.
Each link belongs to exactly two plaquettes with opposite orientation, so
$\partial\theta_p/\partial\theta_\ell\in\{+1,-1,0\}$ and only two terms survive.
Because $h_p$ is itself a function of gauge-invariant inputs, the score is gauge-covariant by construction.

Network capacity, the training budget and the couplings each checkpoint was
trained on, together with the sampler and rethermalization settings used
throughout, are collected in \app{settings}.

\subsection{Reverse Diffusion Process}
\label{sec:reverse_diffusion}

\begin{figure*}[bt]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{reverse_diffusion_schematic.png}
    \caption{The reverse-diffusion chain for 2D U(1) and U(2)
    (\sect{reverse_diffusion}). It runs from right to left, starting from angles
    $\phi_T$ drawn uniformly on the circle and ending at the lifted configuration
    $\phi_0$. Here $s_\theta$ is the learned score and
    $\Delta_t=\sigma_t^2-\sigma_{t-1}^2$, and each denoising step is followed by
    one Langevin correction step (dashed). Before it is handed to HMC, the
    configuration is rethermalized with exact local updates, preceded in U(2) by
    the conditional SU(2) heatbath of \sect{u2-extension}.}
    \label{fig:method_schematic}
\end{figure*}

The wrapped-Gaussian kernel is exactly the transition kernel of a driftless
diffusion wrapped onto the circle, so $f\equiv0$ and
$g(t)=\sqrt{d\sigma_t^2/dt}$: a variance-exploding schedule in the terminology of
\cite{song2021}. The forward process is $d\theta = g(t)dW$ and its reverse-time
companion is
$$d\theta = -g(t)^2\,s_\theta(\theta_t,\sigma_t,\beta)\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar W,$$
so the drift of the reverse process is the learned score. In U(1) one further
term enters: the analytic Wilson score of the target action, evaluated at a
noise-smeared coupling and blended in with a weight that vanishes at large
$\sigma$ and approaches unity at small $\sigma$, so that the network supplies
the drift while the field is noisy and the known action takes over as it becomes
clean. The U(2) sampler uses the learned score alone. Starting from pure noise
on the fine lattice, ancestral sampling integrates this backward through the
noise schedule (\fig{method_schematic}). Every result reported here takes one
Langevin corrector step after each ancestral step \cite{song2021}.

Three mechanisms softly bias $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ toward $Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$
during sampling: the coarse-conditioning channels, the FiLM-embedded global
mean, and a reconstruction-guidance penalty added to the score at each noise
level. That penalty is the gradient, with respect to the cell sum, of
$$-\frac{\big(\sum_{p=1}^4\theta_{p,\mathrm{fine}} - \theta_{p,\mathrm{coarse}}\big)^2}
        {2\lambda(\sigma)}, \qquad \lambda(\sigma)\approx 8\sigma^2,$$
in which $\lambda(\sigma)$ is the noise that the eight boundary links of a coarse
cell inject into the blocked plaquette at level $\sigma$. The per-cell scalar is broadcast to the four fine sub-plaquettes and
assembled into link space by the same discrete curl used for the score head, so
the guidance is gauge-covariant by construction. None of these make $Q_{\mathrm{fine}}=Q_{\mathrm{coarse}}$ exact, they only
make the sampler more likely to settle in the correct sector on its own.

Exactness comes instead from a fourth step: the sampler computes
$\Delta Q = Q_{\mathrm{coarse}} - Q_{\mathrm{fine}}$ and adds $\Delta Q$ copies
of the instanton field of \sect{instanton}. It does this once every ten
reverse-SDE steps below $\sigma\approx0.5$, the level at which $Q$ becomes a
well-defined quantity of the noisy sample, and once more unconditionally after
sampling completes. Applying the projection partway through the reverse
process, rather than only at the end, lets the remaining diffusion steps and the
rethermalization tail relax the ultraviolet error it leaves behind; the final
unconditional application fixes the delivered configuration's topology however
close the soft mechanisms got on their own.

\subsection{Extension to U(2)}
\label{sec:u2-extension}
\label{sec:instanton_apply}

The construction carries over to U(2) essentially unchanged. The lifting step is a
conditional diffusion model that generates only the determinant sector $\psi$,
with $\psi$ standing in for $\theta$ throughout. Integrating SU(2) out of one
plaquette against Haar measure is the standard one-link integral
\cite{creutz1980}, and gives the exact marginal weight of the determinant-sector
plaquette angle $\alpha$,
$$w_{\det}(\alpha;\beta) = \frac{2I_1(z)}{z},
\qquad z=\beta\cos(\alpha/2).$$
This is not of Wilson form, so the U(1) coupling $\beta_1$ that the network is
conditioned on is fixed by the minimum-KL projection of \sect{naive-blocking},

matching of \Eq{topology-matching}, and the difference in criterion follows the
difference in job. The ladder coupling fixes which fine theory a lift targets,
and the charge it transports cannot be corrected downstream, so it is matched on
$\langle Q^2\rangle$. The coupling $\beta_1$ only tells the network which
distribution its input is drawn from; it cannot affect topology, since $Q$ is a
functional of $\psi$ alone and is imposed by transport, and what it does affect,
the local structure, is what the rethermalization tail repairs. It is therefore
matched on the local weight rather than on a global one. Matching
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ here instead is possible and changes little: the two

three rungs of the deployed ladder, reaching $1.4\%$ only at the weakest

\subsection{Cost accounting}
\label{sec:cost}

Although HMC is ergodic by construction, as \sect{reverse_diffusion} notes, within
a finite budget it need not behave as though it were: at these couplings a chain
does not change $Q$ once, so it explores only the distribution conditioned on the
sector it started in, and an interval measured there counts decorrelation within
that sector rather than a supply of independent configurations of the target.
The $t_{\rm interval}$ measurement therefore counts only where the chain has
equilibrated.
Local observables are unbiased inside that sector and decorrelate quickly there,
which is why a plaquette interval looks short on precisely the arms that are
worst off; the ensemble average is nonetheless wrong, by the amount the sector
weight is wrong. A short interval measured on such a chain is not a supply of
independent configurations of the target, so where a classical arm does not
equilibrate within the budget we take its cost as infinite and report $F$ as a
lower bound, that bound being the coupling's own trajectory budget divided by
the preconditioned cost. The budget is $400$ trajectories up to
$\beta\approx90$ and $200$ or $150$ above it, and each bound is quoted against
the run that produced it. What $F$ counts on both sides is HMC trajectories; the cost of
building the preconditioned configuration in the first place falls outside that
count and is treated separately in \app{overhead}.

Two classical baselines are separated because they behave differently.
Plain HMC is the generic sampler, and by $\beta\approx25$ its topology no longer
moves. HMC augmented with the marginal winding move of
\sect{instanton_updates_classical}, which changes $Q$ by one and therefore
reaches both parity classes, is the strongest classical sampler and holds much
further. It is the winding baseline throughout this section and in
\fig{money}.

\fig{money} measures the improvement factor against the winding baseline across
the coupling range, at both evaluated volumes, and the answer has two regimes.

Below $\beta\approx90$ the winding baseline equilibrates, and there the factor
is measured rather than bounded: twelve such cases, spanning six distinct
couplings across the two checkpoints of \fig{money} and the two volumes. It is
modest, with a median of $1.1$, a range of $0.1$ to $12$, and a value below one
in five of the twelve. Counted in trajectories alone and against the strongest classical
sampler this theory admits, the preconditioner is break-even at best, which is
the expected result in a regime where a good classical sampler already exists.

Above $\beta\approx90$ no classical arm reaches equilibrium within the budget
at any coupling tested, so by the gate of \sect{cost} its cost is infinite and
the factor is a lower bound, running from $1.5$ to $200$ with a median of $5.4$.
What is measured there is failure to equilibrate within the budget rather than a
frozen chain: the winding arm's interval remains between $4$ and $45$
trajectories at every coupling except the largest at $L=32$, where $Q^2$ ceases
to move and the interval is unbounded, and it is the bias rather than the
decorrelation that fails to settle. Only for plain HMC, whose topology does
not move at all, is the cost infinite at any budget. For the winding arm it is
not topology that fails: the move keeps firing, and even at $\beta=1660$ it
holds $\langle Q^2\rangle$ between $0.79$ and $1.25$ times exact
(\tb{u2_L128}); it is the local observables that never settle.

\subsection{Reach in volume}
\label{sec:multi-lift}

Reaching a target $(L,\beta)$ may take several lifts, and the error does not
compound when it does (\app{multilift}): it enters at the last rung, so
endpoint accuracy is set by that rung's distance from training coverage and not
by the number of rungs climbed. Laddering therefore extends the volume reached rather than the
coupling, since each lift multiplies $\beta$ by about four and the last one lands
at the same fine coupling whatever path reached it.

The ladder was pushed one rung past the deployed configuration, to $L=128$ at
$\beta=1660.08$, four times the largest linear size in the training set and
sixteen times the sites (\fig{ladder}). Before a single trajectory the
preconditioned configuration sits $0.8$~ppm from the exact plaquette against a
cold start's $1200$~ppm, and it reaches equilibrium in $25$--$30$ trajectories. Neither classical arm reaches equilibrium at all within
the budget, so the improvement factor there is a lower bound.

\section{Training coverage}
\label{sec:coverage}

Where the preconditioner degrades is governed by coupling coverage: which
couplings a checkpoint was trained on, and how far the test coupling sits from
them. This section measures how far that reach extends, and what widening the
training range recovers.

A checkpoint's \emph{training ceiling} is the largest coupling at which it was
ever given training data, so a test coupling above it is an extrapolation and
one below it an interpolation between rungs it has seen. It is a property of the
training set, not of performance. Because the network is conditioned on the fine
side of each training pair, the ceiling is the largest coupling a checkpoint was
asked to generate \emph{at}, with the coarse side near $\beta_f/4$; ceilings are
quoted in raw $\beta$ throughout, matching \fig{money}.

A checkpoint can be trained at a coupling where the classical sampler does not
deliver, because an ensemble is built once and can afford a burn-in no deployed
chain could (\app{settings}). That settles local structure but not topology, so
the charge content is manufactured by the augmentation mechanism of
\sect{naive-blocking}, which consults no closed
form. \SP{\sout{Should we even mention referencing exact $P(Q)$ if we don't use it? It might give readers the wrong idea}} Wrong sector frequencies cost nothing downstream, since the network is
asked only for local structure at a given charge and the charge itself is
transported at deployment.

The experiment in this section is retraining on different sets of coupling
rungs. \texttt{default}, \texttt{wide} and \texttt{wide\_dense} share
architecture, capacity, optimisation and data recipe and differ only in which
fixed rungs their training sets contain, so any change among them is
attributable to coverage alone; \texttt{cov60} differs additionally as noted
below. Four U(2) checkpoints are compared: \SP{We need to fix the cov60 results once ready} \texttt{cov60} and \texttt{default}
are narrow, with ceilings at $\beta=227$ and $416$, \texttt{default} being the
one deployed for every other result here; \texttt{wide} extends the ceiling to
$\beta=2000$ using $23$ rungs against \texttt{default}'s $11$; and
\texttt{wide\_dense} adds $31$ further rungs inside that range without extending
it. \texttt{default}'s rungs are not spread over the coupling axis but placed on
the ladder: its training couplings are $\beta=28$, $105.651$ and $416.524$,
which the deployed ladder of \sect{naive-blocking} visits, together with their
neighbours in the factor-of-four chain, each supplied at the smallest lattice
that teaches the map. It is a ladder-specific training set, and what this section
asks is what such a checkpoint is worth away from its own ladder.
The U(1) set is built the same way, with ceilings $\beta=60$ and $2000$.
Each is scored over a grid held fixed across checkpoints, listed in
\app{grid}, and run twice \SP{\sout{Should we have an appendix that shows what grid this is?}}, once
with plain HMC in every arm and once with the winding update, so preconditioned
and classical starts are always paired under an identical sampler.
\fig{money} draws \texttt{default} and \texttt{wide\_dense}; all four are
tabulated in \app{tables} with the matching U(1) retrain.

Range is the axis that moves the coupling at which the method stops working.
\texttt{default} stops equilibrating
above $\beta=415$ at $L=32$ and above $\beta=94$ at $L=64$, while
\texttt{wide\_dense} works at every coupling tested, to $\beta=1623$ and
$\beta=1660$ respectively. \texttt{wide} does not: at $L=64$ it fails at three couplings inside its own
trained range, $\beta=174$, $637$ and $1660$ under the winding sampler and
$\beta=94$, $637$ and $1660$ under the plain one, so range alone does not
guarantee that the coupling reached moves with it. Density acts elsewhere, and much more weakly. Adding $31$ rungs inside
\texttt{wide}'s existing range improves it at $30$ of $44$ couplings, but by a
median of $2.1\times$ where range gives one to two orders of magnitude. Pooled
over all $44$ cases that gain is resolved, $[1.2,2.7]$, while in either half
alone the interval spans unity, so the effect is small and is resolved only in the combined sample. In U(1) density changes nothing measurable at all.
\tb{u2_coverage_tests} carries both comparisons, with the caveats that qualify
their size. The two axes therefore act where the other does not, and on the
evidence here a fixed rung budget is better spent extending the range than
resampling what it already covers.

The curves in \fig{money} are irregular because $t_{\rm therm}$ is a discrete
first-crossing index, quantized by the spacing of the measurements and shifted
by a single lagging chain out of $64$; adjacent couplings can therefore differ by an order
of magnitude, and the level of a curve carries more than any point on it. Above
$\beta\approx90$ every point is also a lower bound, since no classical arm
equilibrates there and the plotted value is that coupling's budget divided by
the preconditioned cost alone: the peak at $L=64$, $\beta=174$ is one of these,
where the lift arrives already equilibrated against $t_{\rm therm}$ of $44$ and
$84$ trajectories at its neighbours. That there is no clean trend in $\beta$ is itself the
result: the factor tracks distance to the nearest training rung, and the rungs
sit at fixed, unevenly spaced couplings, so that distance falls and rises again
as $\beta$ passes each in turn.

Two limits bound what this grid can show. The first is geometric. The grid holds
$L$ fixed while raising $\beta$, so every step to a higher coupling is also a
step to a smaller physical volume, and the charge distribution narrows with it:
at $L=32$ the exact $\chi_t V$ falls from $0.22$ at $\beta=415$ to $0.0008$ at
$\beta=1623$, where $999$ configurations in $1000$ carry $Q=0$ and almost no charge remains
to be transported. The ladder avoids this by raising $L$ and
$\beta$ together, holding the physical volume and with it $\chi_t V$ nearly
fixed from rung to rung (\sect{naive-blocking}), so $P(Q)$ at the top of the
ladder is essentially that of its base. Higher couplings at fixed physical
volume are therefore reached by climbing (\fig{ladder}), not by extending the
grid.

That caps the grid at $\beta\approx1660$, which is below the wide checkpoints'
ceiling of $2000$, so no test coupling ever asks them to extrapolate. Only the
narrow pair is tested outside its trained range, and there it fails, at all
seven couplings above \texttt{default}'s ceiling. The comparison therefore
establishes that a checkpoint fails outside its own coverage; what it cannot
establish is that widening helps for any reason beyond bringing the test back
inside coverage, since the wide checkpoints were never asked to leave it.
Separating the two would need a grid above $\beta=2000$, at $L\approx200$ to
keep the physical volume from collapsing, beyond what was run here.
\SP{\sout{We definitely need to explain why different training coverages perform so differently. Did we change anything in the training itself?}}
The mechanism behind this sensitivity is the conditioning rather than the
capacity. All four checkpoints have the same architecture and the same
$403{,}394$ parameters, and were trained with the same optimiser, batch size and
epoch budget; the coupling enters the network only through the FiLM embedding of
\sect{scoring-network}, and no other component depends on it. A test coupling
far from every training rung therefore asks the network to evaluate that
embedding where it was never fit, and the score it returns is that of a
different distribution. The two axes act on the two ways this can happen: range
determines whether the test coupling lies inside the interval spanned by the
rungs at all, and density determines how far the nearest rung is once it does.
One qualification applies to the comparison: \texttt{cov60} was trained without
the random symmetry augmentation and the high-$\beta$ noise-sampling bias used
by the other three, so its contrast with \texttt{default} is not a
single-variable experiment. The controlled density comparison is
\texttt{wide\_dense} against \texttt{wide}, which differ by nothing but the
$31$ added rungs.

The second limit is that a ceiling predicts failure only roughly, and errs both
ways: \texttt{cov60} still equilibrates at $\beta=264$, above its own ceiling of
$227$, while \texttt{default} has already failed at $\beta=174$ and $L=64$,
under half of its $416$. The volume step moves that point without harming the
lift, whose deviation is smaller at $L=64$ for all four checkpoints
(\tb{volume_scaling}). What tightens with volume is the bar the chain is held
to, not the error it makes: an ensemble mean over more sites carries a smaller
error bar, so the same bias takes longer to fall inside it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\JH{\sout{(* At the beginning, we should have one sentence to motivate 4D SU(3) saying that our ultimate goal is 4D SU(3) and full QCD with dynamic fermions. *)}}
The object of this programme is four-dimensional SU(3) gauge theory and
ultimately full QCD with dynamical fermions, where topological freezing is a
practical limit on the lattice spacings reachable and where no exact reference
exists to score against. The two-dimensional theories studied here are a
controlled setting in which the method can be measured rather than the
destination.
The results above rest on two structural properties of the two-dimensional
theories: blocking preserves $Q$ exactly, and an inexpensive update exists that
changes it. Pure 4D SU(3) has neither, and differs in four respects that bear on
the method: it is not exactly solvable, it has no trivial blocking map, it has
no trivial instanton update, and its topological charge has no unambiguous
lattice definition.

Transport needs two things, and the blocking map supplies only the first. It
pairs fine and coarse configurations while preserving $Q$ exactly, but only
because the plaquette phase is an abelian sum of link variables, so the four
fine sub-plaquettes telescope into the coarse one; an ordered product of SU(3)
matrices does not telescope. The second requirement is distributional: the
coarse ensemble's $P(Q)$ must already be the fine theory's, since an exact map
applied to a charge from the wrong distribution reproduces the wrong
distribution exactly. In 2D this is free, because the ladder holds $\chi_t V$ approximately
fixed and the deployed rungs inherit it (\sect{naive-blocking}).
In 4D the principle survives at fixed physical volume, but neither the matching
nor the charge itself stays exact: the couplings would have to be matched
numerically by scale setting \cite{sommer1994,luscher2010}, and $Q$ has no cheap
unambiguous lattice definition. The field-theoretic form requires smoothing,
whose scheme and extent measurably affect the result at finite spacing
\cite{alexandrou2015}, while the index of a Ginsparg--Wilson operator is exact
but expensive \cite{hasenfratz1998}.
Imposing $Q_{\rm coarse}$ would then be exact only with respect to a chosen
definition, and the coarse end of the ladder, where discretization artifacts are
largest, is where that choice matters most.

The instanton field is the method's other structural tool, and it serves twice:
it manufactures sector coverage in the training data, and it imposes
$Q_{\rm coarse}$ on the lifted configuration. It exists in U(2) because the
group decomposes into a U(1) factor and a topologically trivial SU(2) factor,
with the charge carried entirely by the abelian piece, so the U(1) construction
transfers directly. SU(3) does not have this property, and a cheap
topology-changing update for it is a central open question for this method. A promising method for more
complex theories is parallel tempering on boundary conditions, introduced
for the two-dimensional $\mathrm{CP}^{N-1}$ model \cite{hasenbusch2017} and
since carried to four-dimensional $SU(N)$ gauge theory and to full QCD
\cite{bonanno2021,bonanno2024}. This manufactures global topological moves for
exactly the theories that lack a cheap analytic one. This method asks less of
such a mechanism than a sampler does: it is needed only while training data is
built, and only to visit sectors, so neither its acceptance rate nor the sector
frequencies it produces need be correct. What the closed form does remain necessary for is \emph{scoring}:
every accuracy statement in this paper is a deviation from it.

Coverage also admits a route cheaper than a topological update, and one we
regard as the more promising of the two. It can be bought by deliberately
biasing the generating action rather than by moving between sectors under the
correct one:
add a term conjugate to the charge, a chemical potential $-\mu Q$ that tilts the
ensemble toward a chosen sector, or a bias potential in $Q$ that flattens the
barriers between sectors, as multicanonical sampling \cite{bergneuhaus1992} and
metadynamics do, and the latter has already been applied to lattice topological freezing
\cite{laio2016}. Ordinarily such a bias has to be undone, by reweighting each
configuration by $e^{+\mu Q}$ or by the accumulated bias potential. Here there
is nothing to undo: the biased run only populates the training set with
configurations at nonzero $Q$, and at deployment the charge is imposed by
transport from the coarse configuration rather than drawn from the model, so
the sector frequencies the biased run produces never enter any result. The model has to learn the local
structure at a given charge, which the biased ensemble supplies correctly at
each $Q$ it visits; how often it visits each one is free.

The division of labour is the part of this method we expect to outlast the
particular architecture used here. Generating UV structure and
preserving IR and topological structure are different jobs, each best done by
the tool suited to it: HMC for the UV, a learned generative model for the
topological content HMC cannot reach on its own. Normalizing flows
\cite{albergo2019}, gauge-equivariant L-CNNs \cite{favoni2020} and other
diffusion architectures \cite{zhu2024,zhu2025,aarts2026} are natural candidates
for extending it to more complicated theories.

\section{Network, training and sampler settings}
\label{app:settings}

The settings are as follows. The U(1) network has $324{,}954$ parameters at
hidden width $56$ and depth $4$; the U(2) one has $403{,}394$ at width $64$ and
the same depth; both use $3\times3$ kernels. Training is denoising score
matching under Adam, for $100$ epochs at batch size $16$ and learning rate
$3\times10^{-4}$ in U(1) and $120$ epochs at batch size $32$ and
$2\times10^{-4}$ in U(2), holding out a tenth of each training set for
validation; the deployed U(2) run stopped at epoch $109$ rather than $120$, and
both checkpoints hold the exponential moving average of the weights at their
best validation epoch. The U(1) objective carries one additional term, a soft
topological-charge penalty on the one-step denoised estimate at weight $0.1$;
the U(2) objective is denoising score matching alone. The
deployed U(2) checkpoint is trained on eleven fixed $(L,\beta)$ rungs spanning
nine distinct couplings, and the deployed U(1) one on four fixed rungs plus $78$
couplings drawn at random below its ceiling; \sect{coverage} varies exactly this list and
nothing else. Sampling uses $200$ ancestral steps, each followed by one
Langevin corrector step, with the charge projection applied every ten steps
below $\sigma=0.5$. The tail is $16$ local sweeps in U(1), and in U(2) $30$
conditional SU(2) heatbath sweeps followed by $10$ local sweeps, a local sweep
being one heatbath pass over the lattice followed by two overrelaxation passes.

The training ensembles themselves are burned in once per rung, at a cost no
deployed chain could afford: $200$ to $2000$ HMC trajectories in U(1), rising
with the coupling, and in U(2) sixty heatbath and microcanonical
overrelaxation \cite{creutz1987} sweeps followed by $150$ to $400$
trajectories.

\section{Topology-matched ladder couplings}
\label{app:matching}

\Eq{topology-matching} has no closed-form solution, so $\beta_f$ is obtained
numerically: $\langle Q^2\rangle$ is evaluated from the character expansion of
\app{exact} at fixed $L$, and the root is bracketed between $\beta_c$ and
$64(\beta_c+1)$ and taken by Brent's method to a tolerance of $10^{-9}$. The
bracket is safe because $\langle Q^2\rangle$ at fixed $L$ decreases
monotonically in $\beta$, and the left end is below the solution for every step
of the form $L_f=2L_c$.

\tb{schedules} compares the resulting schedule with the two alternatives, all
three started from the same base ensemble. Against the deployed schedule's
$1.2\times10^{-5}$, a character-matched ladder from that base leaves a total
variation distance of $5.7\times10^{-3}$ between the top rung's exact $P(Q)$ and
the base's, and the tree-level rule $1.5\times10^{-2}$, with
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ low by $7.1\%$. Matching the plaquette leaves
$\langle Q^2\rangle$ low by $2.2\%$ after one step and $2.8\%$ after two, and
the tree-level rule by $5.8\%$ and $7.1\%$; matching \Eq{topology-matching}
leaves it unchanged to the five digits shown. The cost is a coupling $2$--$3\%$
below the character-matched one, a shift the local observables of
\sect{u2obs} do not resolve.

\section{The coupling grid}
\label{app:grid}

Every checkpoint in \sect{coverage} is scored on the same grid, and each point of
it is an independent lift: a coarse ensemble at $\beta_c$ is lifted to
$\beta_f$ at twice the linear size, and no charge is carried between points.
\tb{grid} lists them. The grid holds $L$ fixed and raises $\beta$, so it moves
along the direction in which the physical volume shrinks and $\chi_t V$ falls,
which is why it is not a ladder and why the invariance of
\Eq{topology-matching} does not apply along it.

The trajectory budget shortens as the coupling rises, since the runs at the top
of the grid exist to establish that no classical arm equilibrates rather than to
measure how quickly one does. Each lower bound in \sect{cost} is quoted against
the budget of the coupling that produced it.

\section{Statistical methodology}
\label{app:stats}

Standard machinery is used as specified in the sources cited where it appears,
and the two timescales are defined in \sect{relaxation-correction}. One
convention is ours.

The resampling unit is the chain. Every error bar on a sampled ensemble quantity
resamples whole chains. Beyond the usual underestimate for an autocorrelated
series \cite{wolff2004}, the case here fails outright: a topologically frozen
chain has zero within-chain variance in $Q$, so pooling over configurations
reports a vanishing error on an arm that has sampled nothing. A generated
ensemble is the exception, since a lift's configurations descend from
independent base chains and are themselves the independent unit; there the
configuration-level bootstrap and the naive standard error agree to within a few
percent, which we verify rather than assume.

\section{Error accumulation over multiple lifts}
\label{app:multilift}

Because a target may be reached by more than one lift, the error a single lift
introduces must not accumulate along the chain of them, and it does not. Across
eight cases, comprising both theories, an endpoint inside and one beyond the
training range, and intermediate rethermalization between rungs enabled and
disabled, three lifts to a fixed endpoint leave $0.84$--$1.02$ times the
deviation from exact that a single lift to the same endpoint leaves. The error
is instead introduced by the final rung, which is why endpoint accuracy tracks that rung's
distance from training coverage rather than the length of the ladder below it.

\section{Cost of building a preconditioned configuration}
\label{app:overhead}

The improvement factor of \Eq{improvement-factor} counts HMC trajectories in
both its numerator and its denominator. One cost falls outside that count:
producing the preconditioned configuration in the first place, which takes a
base ensemble at the bottom of the ladder plus one reverse diffusion per lift.
On the hardware used here that overhead is not small, and $F$ should not be
read as a wall-clock speed-up. In U(1), where the classical baseline still
converges, the one-time campaign that produced the checkpoint costs more than
every classical burn-in that converges, and the marginal cost of a lifted
configuration is larger than a trajectory's as well, so measured in seconds the
method loses at every coupling where the classical baseline is correct. In U(2)
the top rung of the ladder costs roughly twice what a winding-HMC configuration
costs, dominated by the reverse-diffusion sampler, whose step count is set for
accuracy and not tuned for speed.

What that does not do is change the comparison, for two reasons. The base
ensemble is generated once, at a coupling where sampling is cheap, and is
amortized over every configuration lifted from it; and the reverse diffusion is
a fixed number of network evaluations, the same at every coupling, while
$t_{\rm interval}$ in the numerator grows without bound as the classical chain
freezes. A fixed overhead cannot close a gap that diverges, and above
$\beta\approx90$, where no classical arm equilibrates at all, there is no finite
quantity for it to close. The case for the method is therefore the regime where
the classical baseline ceases to be correct at any budget, together with the
independence of the generative cost from $\beta$, and not a wall-clock advantage
in the regime where that baseline still applies.
